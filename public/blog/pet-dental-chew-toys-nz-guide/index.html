Dental Chew Toys & Oral Health for New Zealand Pets

Published 30 July 2026 · ZiggySitters NZ

By age three, over 80% of dogs and 70% of cats in New Zealand show signs of dental disease. That is not a scare statistic — it is the reality reported by the New Zealand Veterinary Association. The good news is that most dental disease is preventable, and the tools to prevent it are accessible and affordable. From choosing the right chew toys to establishing a brushing routine, this guide covers everything NZ pet owners need to know about keeping their pets' teeth clean, their gums healthy, and their breath tolerable.

Why Pet Dental Health Matters More Than You Think

Dental disease in pets is not just about bad breath. Left untreated, it leads to pain, difficulty eating, tooth loss, and even organ damage. Bacteria from infected gums enter the bloodstream and can affect the heart, kidneys, and liver. For older pets, this is a serious and often hidden health risk.

The progression is predictable and preventable:

  1. Plaque forms within hours of eating — a soft, sticky film of bacteria
  2. Tartar develops when plaque mineralises — this hardened deposit cannot be brushed away
  3. Gingivitis is gum inflammation caused by tartar and bacteria — gums become red, swollen, and prone to bleeding
  4. Periodontal disease damages the ligaments and bone holding teeth in place — leading to loose teeth, abscesses, and pain

Each stage is harder and more expensive to treat. Prevention is genuinely cheaper than cure — a dental chew costs a few dollars; a professional dental procedure under anaesthesia costs hundreds to over a thousand.

Understanding the NZ Pet Dental Market

New Zealand pet owners have excellent access to dental care products, but the sheer variety can be overwhelming. Here is what you need to know about what is available locally:

What to Look For

  • VOHC approval. The Veterinary Oral Health Council (VOHC) awards its seal to products that have been scientifically proven to reduce plaque and tartar. Look for the seal on packaging — it is the most reliable quality indicator.
  • NZ-sourced ingredients. Products made with NZ lamb, venison, or beef are widely available and often higher quality than imported alternatives.
  • Appropriate hardness. A chew should be firm enough to require sustained chewing but not so hard that it fractures teeth. The thumbnail test: if you cannot make a dent with your thumbnail, it is too hard for your dog.
  • Size-appropriate. Chews that are too small are choking hazards; too large and your pet may lose interest before getting dental benefit.

Best Dental Chew Toys for Dogs in NZ

Rubber Chew Toys

The gold standard for dental maintenance. Rubber toys flex against teeth and gums, providing a cleaning action as the dog chews. Look for:

  • KONG Classic and KONG Extreme: Available at all major NZ pet stores. Fill with wet food or dental paste and freeze for extended chewing. The Extreme version is suitable for power chewers.
  • KONG Dental Stick: Specifically designed with ridges that clean teeth as the dog chews. More targeted dental benefit than the classic shape.
  • West Paw Zogoflex: Made from recyclable materials, dishwasher-safe, and backed by a one-time replacement guarantee. Available in NZ through specialty pet retailers.

Natural Dental Chews

New Zealand produces some of the best natural dental chews in the world:

  • Deer antlers (NZ red deer): Long-lasting, naturally shed, and rich in minerals. Choose split antlers for moderate chewers and whole antlers for power chewers. Available from NZ pet retailers and directly from South Island farms.
  • Greenies: The most widely VOHC-approved dental chew. Available in size-specific options at vet clinics and pet stores nationwide.
  • Whimzees: Plant-based dental chews that are grain-free and suitable for dogs with food sensitivities. Available at most NZ pet stores.
  • Kiwi Kissers (NZ-made): Made from 100% NZ green-lipped mussel and fish. These are both a dental chew and a joint supplement — uniquely Kiwi.
  • DentaFun NZ: Locally produced dental sticks using NZ-sourced ingredients. Available online and in independent pet stores.

Rope Toys and Textured Toys

Rope toys provide a flossing action as dogs chew on the strands. They are not a complete dental solution but make a useful addition to your dental toolkit. Choose tightly braided cotton rope toys and replace them when they become frayed — loose strands can be swallowed.

Warning: Never give your dog cooked bones, antlers that splinter, or hard nylon bones. Cooked bones splinter and can perforate the digestive tract. Oversized or excessively hard chews fracture teeth — the most common cause of slab fractures in dogs.

Dental Care for Cats

Cats are notoriously underserved when it comes to dental care, partly because they resist oral handling and partly because owners assume dry food keeps teeth clean. It does not — most dry kibble shatters on contact and never touches the gumline where disease starts.

Chew Options for Cats

  • Feline Greenies: The only VOHC-approved dental treat for cats. Available in chicken, salmon, and tuna flavours at NZ pet stores and vet clinics.
  • Dental kibble formulations: Prescription dental diets (such as Hill's t/d) have larger kibble pieces that require more chewing. These are available through your vet, not the supermarket.
  • Raw chicken necks or wings: A natural option many NZ cat owners use. The chewing action does help clean teeth, but there is a small risk of bacterial contamination and choking. Discuss with your vet first.
  • Cat-specific chew toys: Small rubber toys infused with catnip can encourage chewing behaviour. Not a dental solution on their own, but they contribute to oral stimulation.

Brushing a Cat's Teeth

It is possible — and worth the effort. Start with finger brushes or gauze wrapped around your finger, using cat-specific enzymatic toothpaste (never human toothpaste). Gradually introduce the toothbrush over two to three weeks. Even brushing two to three times per week makes a measurable difference in plaque reduction.

How to Brush Your Pet's Teeth: Step by Step

Brushing is the single most effective thing you can do for your pet's dental health. Here is how to build the habit:

Week 1: Getting Comfortable

  • Let your pet sniff and taste the pet toothpaste from your finger
  • Gently lift their lips and touch their teeth and gums with your finger
  • Keep sessions to 30 seconds and reward with praise and treats

Week 2: Introducing the Brush

  • Put toothpaste on a finger brush or pet toothbrush
  • Brush the outside surfaces of the front teeth only
  • Gradually work towards the back teeth over several days
  • Aim for 60 seconds of brushing

Week 3 and Beyond: Full Routine

  • Brush all outer surfaces of the teeth — the tongue side cleans itself through rough contact
  • Use small circular motions along the gumline
  • Aim for daily brushing, but even three times per week is effective
  • Make it part of your evening routine so it becomes habit for both of you
Ziggy Tip: Pet toothpaste comes in flavours like chicken, beef, and seafood. Finding the right flavour can transform brushing from a struggle to something your pet actually looks forward to. Most NZ vet clinics stock sample sizes — ask for a few to test.

Water Additives and Rinses

Dental water additives are an easy supplementary measure. You add a measured amount to your pet's drinking water each day. They do not replace brushing, but they reduce bacterial load and help control plaque between brushings.

Popular options available in NZ include:

  • Healthy Mouth: VOHC-approved water additive available through vet clinics
  • Aquadent: Available at most pet stores, helps reduce plaque formation
  • OraVet Rinse: Applied directly to gums after brushing, creates a barrier that prevents bacteria from adhering to teeth

These are particularly useful for pets who absolutely will not tolerate brushing — they provide partial benefit with zero stress.

Signs Your Pet Needs Veterinary Dental Care

Watch for these red flags that indicate your pet needs professional attention, not just home care:

  • Persistent bad breath — not just "dog breath" but foul-smelling odour that does not improve with brushing
  • Red or bleeding gums — healthy gums should be pink, not red or swollen
  • Difficulty eating — dropping food, chewing on one side only, or avoiding hard food
  • Excessive drooling — especially if drool has blood in it
  • Pawing at the mouth — indicates oral pain
  • Loose or broken teeth — visible damage or teeth that move when touched
  • Swelling around the face or jaw — could indicate a dental abscess
  • Sudden change in behaviour — irritability, withdrawal, or reluctance to be touched around the head
Important: If your pet has any of these signs, book a vet appointment. Dental disease is painful, and pets are excellent at hiding pain. Do not wait for it to get worse — it will not improve on its own.

Professional Dental Procedures: What to Expect in NZ

When home care is not enough, your vet will recommend a professional dental procedure. Here is what that typically involves in New Zealand:

Cost Range (2026)

  • Standard dental clean (scale and polish): $350-$600 depending on clinic and location
  • Dental clean with extractions: $600-$1,500 depending on the number and complexity of extractions
  • Specialist dental work (root canals, orthodontics): $1,500-$3,000+ at referral centres in Auckland, Wellington, and Christchurch

What Happens During the Procedure

  • Pre-anaesthetic blood work to check organ function
  • General anaesthesia — necessary because pets will not hold still for dental work
  • Full mouth examination and dental X-rays (70% of dental disease is below the gumline)
  • Ultrasonic scaling to remove tartar above and below the gumline
  • Polishing to smooth the tooth surface and slow future plaque accumulation
  • Extractions if necessary — followed by pain management
  • Post-procedure home care plan to prevent recurrence

Pet Insurance and Dental Cover

Not all NZ pet insurance policies cover dental work. Check your policy carefully — many exclude dental disease as a "pre-existing condition" or require you to have maintained a dental care routine to qualify for claims. Policies from providers like Petcover, PD Insurance, and Southern Cross Pet Insurance vary in their dental coverage, so compare before you buy.

A Dental Care Routine That Actually Works

Consistency beats perfection. Here is a realistic weekly routine for the average NZ pet owner:

Daily

  • Brush teeth for 60-90 seconds (or at least 3-4 times per week)
  • Provide one dental chew appropriate to your pet's size
  • Add dental water additive to drinking water

Weekly

  • Check your pet's mouth for red gums, broken teeth, or unusual lumps
  • Wash rubber chew toys in the dishwasher to prevent bacterial buildup
  • Replace worn or damaged chew toys

Every 6-12 Months

  • Vet dental check-up — most NZ vets include a basic oral exam in annual health checks
  • Professional dental clean if recommended
  • Replace toothbrush (bristles wear out just like human toothbrushes)

Dental Care When You Are Away: What Pet Sitters Need to Know

If your pet requires dental care — whether that is daily brushing, dental chews at specific times, or medication for ongoing dental issues — your pet sitter needs clear instructions. Here is what to prepare:

  • Written dental routine: List exactly what needs to happen, in what order, and at what time of day
  • Toothbrush and toothpaste: Leave the specific products your pet is used to — do not expect the sitter to improvise
  • Dental chew supply: Leave enough for the entire period, plus a few extras
  • Warning signs: Tell your sitter what to watch for — bad breath, drooling, refusing food, or pawing at the mouth
  • Vet contact: Provide your vet's name, phone number, and after-hours emergency details
  • Permission for emergency care: If you are overseas, give your sitter written authorisation to seek emergency veterinary care and leave a credit card on file with your vet

Common Mistakes NZ Pet Owners Make With Dental Care

  1. Relying on dry food alone. Dry kibble does not clean teeth — most pieces shatter on contact. Dental-specific diets are different from regular dry food.
  2. Using human toothpaste. Fluoride and xylitol are toxic to pets. Always use pet-specific enzymatic toothpaste.
  3. Starting too late. Puppies and kittens should have their mouths handled from a young age. Waiting until dental disease is established makes brushing much harder.
  4. Giving bones that are too hard. Marrow bones, cooked bones, and some antlers can fracture teeth. The dental chew industry exists because bones are a risky solution.
  5. Skipping the vet check. Annual dental exams catch problems early when they are cheap and simple to treat. Waiting until your pet stops eating means waiting until the problem is severe and expensive.
  6. Inconsistent routines. Brushing once a month does almost nothing. Three times per week is the minimum for meaningful plaque reduction.

Breed-Specific Dental Considerations

Some breeds common in New Zealand are predisposed to dental problems:

  • Small breeds (Chihuahua, Maltese, Pug, Pomeranian): Overcrowded teeth, early tartar buildup, and retained baby teeth. Dental care is non-negotiable for these breeds.
  • Brachycephalic breeds (French Bulldog, Pug, British Bulldog): Misaligned jaws trap food and accelerate tartar. Professional cleaning is often needed annually.
  • Greyhounds: Notoriously prone to dental disease. Many ex-racing greyhounds adopted in NZ need immediate dental work.
  • Persian and exotic cats: Their flat faces create the same overcrowding issues as brachycephalic dogs. Regular dental checks are essential.
  • Border Collies and working dogs: Fractured teeth from chewing sticks or stones are common. Check for chips and cracks regularly.

If you have one of these breeds, dental care is not optional — it is a core part of responsible ownership. Budget for annual dental checks and build brushing into your routine from day one.

The Bottom Line

Dental care is one of the highest-ROI investments you can make in your pet's health. A consistent routine of brushing, appropriate chews, and annual vet checks prevents painful disease, avoids expensive procedures, and adds years of quality life. Start small — even a finger brush three times per week is better than nothing — and build from there. Your pet's teeth, gums, and overall health will thank you.
