Online Vet and Telehealth Services in New Zealand: A Complete Guide

2026-07-31 · 10 min read · ZiggySitters Team

Getting veterinary advice at 11pm on a Sunday when your dog has eaten something suspicious, or your cat has started liming, used to mean a costly after-hours emergency visit or an anxious wait until Monday. Online vet and telehealth services have changed that. New Zealand now has a growing number of platforms that connect pet owners with registered veterinarians via video call, phone, or chat, offering professional guidance when and where you need it.

This guide explains what online vet services are available in New Zealand, what they can and cannot do, how much they cost, and when you still need to see a vet in person.

What Is Veterinary Telehealth?

Veterinary telehealth covers any remote interaction between a pet owner and a veterinary professional. In New Zealand, this typically falls into three categories:

  • Teletriage — Assessing whether a situation is urgent and directing you to the right level of care. This is the most common use case and arguably the most valuable. A triage vet can tell you whether your pet needs to see an emergency clinic tonight or can safely wait until morning.
  • Teleconsultation — A full video or phone consultation with a registered vet who can assess symptoms, provide advice, and in some cases prescribe medication. This works best for non-emergency conditions where a physical examination is not strictly necessary.
  • Telemonitoring — Ongoing remote check-ins for chronic conditions, post-surgical recovery, or medication management. Your regular vet may offer this for follow-up appointments.

It is important to understand that in New Zealand, veterinary telehealth is regulated by the Veterinary Council of New Zealand (VCNZ). Registered veterinarians providing telehealth services must meet the same professional standards as in-person practitioners, and they cannot prescribe restricted drugs without having established a valid veterinary-client-patient relationship (VCPR).

Online Vet Services Available in New Zealand

The telehealth landscape for pets in New Zealand has expanded significantly. Here are the main options:

VetChat

VetChat is one of the most well-known online veterinary services in New Zealand. It connects pet owners with NZ-registered vets via video call or chat, available seven days a week including evenings. VetChat veterinarians can assess symptoms, provide triage advice, recommend treatment plans, and help you decide whether an in-person visit is necessary. They cannot prescribe all medications remotely but can advise on over-the-counter options and recommend when a physical examination is required.

PawPals Vet

PawPals offers subscription-based virtual vet consultations alongside in-person services at their Auckland clinic. Subscribers get unlimited chat consultations and discounted video calls. This model works well for pet owners who want quick answers to routine questions without booking a full appointment.

Your Regular Vet is Telehealth Option

Many New Zealand veterinary clinics now offer phone or video consultations as an add-on to their in-person services. This is often the best option because your regular vet already knows your pet is medical history, has a pre-existing VCPR, and can prescribe medications. Ask your clinic whether they offer remote consultations and what the process looks like.

After-Hours Emergency Lines

Most major NZ cities have after-hours emergency veterinary clinics that offer phone triage. While not full telehealth platforms, these services can assess urgency and guide you on whether to bring your pet in immediately. The Veterinary Emergency Group operates in Auckland, Wellington, and Christchurch, and several independent emergency clinics offer similar phone services.

What Online Vet Services Can Help With

Telehealth is excellent for a wide range of non-emergency situations:

  • Skin conditions — Rashes, hot spots, allergic reactions, and flea concerns can often be assessed via clear photos or video.
  • Minor digestive issues — Mild vomiting, diarrhoea, or appetite changes that are not accompanied by other warning signs.
  • Behavioural concerns — Anxiety, changes in behaviour, separation distress, and general advice on training approaches.
  • Nutrition and diet — Food recommendations, weight management advice, and feeding schedules for different life stages.
  • Post-surgical follow-up — Checking incision sites, monitoring recovery, and assessing whether concerns are normal or need further attention.
  • Medication questions — Clarifying dosing instructions, discussing side effects, or checking whether over-the-counter products are appropriate.
  • Triage — The single most valuable telehealth function: deciding whether your pet needs emergency care tonight or can wait until the morning.
  • Second opinions — Getting another perspective on a diagnosis or treatment plan from your regular vet.

What Online Vet Services Cannot Do

Telehealth has clear limitations. Understanding these prevents disappointment and, more importantly, prevents dangerous delays:

  • Physical examinations — A vet cannot palpate an abdomen, listen to the heart with a stethoscope, or check joint mobility through a screen. Many diagnoses require hands-on assessment.
  • Emergency care — If your pet is struggling to breathe, has a suspected fracture, is experiencing seizures, has severe bleeding, or is unresponsive, go to an emergency vet immediately. Do not wait for a telehealth appointment.
  • Prescribing restricted medications — NZ law requires a valid VCPR for prescribing many medications. A first-time video call may not meet this threshold unless the vet already has a relationship with your pet.
  • Diagnostics — Blood tests, X-rays, ultrasound, and other diagnostics require in-person visits. A telehealth vet can only recommend which tests may be needed.
  • Vaccinations — These must be administered in person, and most NZ boarding facilities and daycares require vaccination certificates from an in-person vet.

Costs of Online Vet Services in New Zealand

One of the main attractions of telehealth is cost. Here is what you can expect to pay:

  • Chat-based consultations — Typically $20 to $40 per session. Good for quick questions and triage.
  • Video consultations — Typically $45 to $90 per session, depending on the platform and duration. Some platforms offer 15-minute consultations at the lower end.
  • Subscription plans — Monthly plans from $15 to $30 that include unlimited chat and discounted video calls. Good value if you have a pet with ongoing health questions.
  • After-hours phone triage — Usually free through emergency vet clinics, though they will direct you to come in if the situation warrants it.

For comparison, a standard in-person vet consultation in New Zealand costs between $50 and $90, with after-hours emergency consults starting at $150 to $250 plus treatment costs. Telehealth can save you money by helping you avoid unnecessary emergency visits, but it is not a replacement for regular veterinary care.

Is Pet Insurance Worth It for Telehealth?

Several NZ pet insurance providers now cover telehealth consultations, either as part of their standard policy or as an add-on. Petplan, Southern Cross Pet Insurance, and AA Pet Insurance all offer policies that include virtual vet visits. Check your policy details, as coverage varies between providers and plans.

Even without insurance coverage, a $45 video consultation that prevents an unnecessary $200 emergency visit pays for itself many times over. The real value of telehealth is not in replacing your regular vet but in providing professional guidance at the moment you need it most.

Preparing for an Online Vet Consultation

To get the most out of a telehealth appointment, preparation matters:

  1. Have your pet is medical history ready — Vaccination records, previous diagnoses, current medications, and any recent test results.
  2. Take clear photos and videos beforehand — If your pet has a visible issue (skin rash, limp, wound), photograph or video it before the call. Good lighting and close-up shots make a significant difference.
  3. Note specific symptoms and timing — When did the symptom start? Has it changed? What did your pet eat in the last 24 hours? The more precise you can be, the better the vet can assess the situation.
  4. Be in a quiet, well-lit space — Background noise and poor lighting make video consultations much harder for the vet.
  5. Have your pet nearby — The vet may ask to see your pet move, breathe, or react during the call.
  6. Write down your questions — Consultations are time-limited. Prioritise your concerns so the most important ones get addressed first.

When to Use Telehealth vs. In-Person Vet Visits

Use Telehealth For:

  • Non-urgent skin irritations or rashes
  • Mild digestive upset (single episode of vomiting, soft stool)
  • Behavioural questions and general advice
  • Diet and nutrition guidance
  • Post-surgical wound checks (in addition to, not replacing, scheduled in-person check-ups)
  • Triage: deciding if an after-hours visit is needed
  • Medication questions for conditions already diagnosed by your regular vet

Go to the Vet In Person For:

  • Difficulty breathing or persistent coughing
  • Suspected fractures or inability to bear weight
  • Seizures or collapse
  • Severe bleeding or deep wounds
  • Bloated or distended abdomen (especially in deep-chested breeds, this is a veterinary emergency)
  • Ingestion of known toxins (karaka berries, rat bait, chocolate, lilies for cats)
  • Eyes that are swollen, red, or producing discharge
  • Straining to urinate (especially in male cats, this is a medical emergency)
  • Vaccinations and annual health checks
  • Any situation where your pet is in obvious, significant pain

The Future of Veterinary Telehealth in New Zealand

Telehealth for pets in New Zealand is still evolving. The Veterinary Council of New Zealand continues to update guidelines as technology and demand grow. We can expect to see more integration between telehealth platforms and physical clinics, remote monitoring devices (like smart collars that track vital signs), and expanded prescribing capabilities as regulations adapt.

For rural New Zealand, where access to veterinary care can mean a two-hour drive each way, telehealth is not just convenient, it is essential. For urban pet owners, it is a practical first step that can save time, money, and worry. The best approach is to see telehealth as complementary to your regular vet, not a replacement for it.

Conclusion

Online vet and telehealth services in New Zealand give pet owners access to professional veterinary advice at any time, from anywhere in the country. They are ideal for triage, non-emergency concerns, behavioural questions, and nutritional guidance. They cannot replace hands-on examinations, emergency care, or vaccinations, but they fill a genuine gap in the pet care landscape.

The smartest approach is to register with a telehealth service before you need it, share access details with your pet sitter, and always know where your nearest emergency clinic is. That combination of online convenience and in-person readiness gives your pet the best possible care at every hour.
