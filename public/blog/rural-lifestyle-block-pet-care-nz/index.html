Rural & Lifestyle Block Pet Care in New Zealand: A Complete Guide

2026-08-01 · 12 min read · ZiggySitters Team

New Zealanders love their lifestyle blocks. From the rolling hills of the Waikato to the rugged beauty of Central Otago, thousands of Kiwi families have swapped city living for the space, freedom, and connection to the land that comes with a rural property. But life on a lifestyle block brings a different kind of pet care challenge. You are not just looking after a cat and a dog — you are managing working dogs, chooks, maybe a few sheep, and a cat that refuses to come inside. Finding someone trustworthy to look after all of that while you go away? That is a whole different ball game from booking a city pet sitter.

What Makes Rural Pet Care Different

Lifestyle blocks and rural properties in New Zealand present unique pet care challenges that city dwellers rarely consider. Your animals may be spread across several hectares rather than contained in a single house. Working dogs have exercise and mental stimulation needs that far exceed those of companion dogs. Chooks need daily feeding, egg collection, and predator protection. Cats on rural properties are often semi-feral hunters who may not appreciate a stranger trying to coax them inside. And the distances involved mean that emergency veterinary care can be 30 to 60 minutes away rather than just down the road.

Understanding these differences is the first step towards building a reliable pet care plan for your lifestyle block. Whether you are heading away for a long weekend or need regular support during lambing season, having the right systems and sitters in place means your animals stay healthy, safe, and well-cared-for — and you can actually relax while you are away.

Working Dogs: The Heart of Rural NZ

New Zealand working dogs — Heading Dogs, Huntaways, and the hardy crossbreeds that make up the bulk of the farm dog population — are athletes. They cover extraordinary distances daily, respond to complex vocal and whistle commands, and form intense bonds with their handlers. Their care needs are fundamentally different from those of pet dogs, and anyone looking after them needs to understand this.

A working dog that is not exercised properly will become frustrated, destructive, and potentially aggressive. They do not just need a walk around the block — they need structured activity that engages their mind and body. If you are leaving your working dog with a sitter, provide clear instructions about their exercise requirements. Many working dogs are kennelled rather than housed inside, which means the sitter needs to understand kennel routines, chain or run safety, and the importance of consistent feeding and exercise times.

Nutrition for working dogs is also different. These dogs burn thousands of kilojoules a day during peak work periods and need high-protein, high-fat diets to maintain condition. Talk to your vet about the best feeding regime for your working dogs, and ensure your sitter knows exactly what, how much, and when to feed. Sudden changes in diet can cause digestive upset, so leave enough of your regular working dog biscuits or meat to last the entire period you are away.

Chooks: More Than Just Egg Layers

Chooks are the gateway animal of the New Zealand lifestyle block. Almost every rural property has a coop, and for good reason — fresh eggs, pest control, and endless entertainment. But chooks need consistent daily care, and this is where many lifestyle block pet sitters fall short.

Daily chook care includes letting them out of the coop in the morning, checking feed and water, collecting eggs, and securing them in the coop at dusk to protect from predators. New Zealand has a healthy population of stoats, ferrets, weasels, and feral cats — all of which will happily take chooks given the opportunity. A good sitter needs to be diligent about coop security, especially during dusk when predators are most active.

Chooks also need their coop cleaned regularly, and their health monitored. Respiratory issues, mites, and egg-binding are common problems. Leave your sitter a list of signs to watch for, the contact details of a poultry-knowledgeable vet, and clear instructions about what to do if a chook appears unwell. If your chooks are free-ranging, make sure the sitter knows the boundaries of their usual range and any plants or areas they should be kept away from.

Rural Cats: The Independent Hunters

Cats on lifestyle blocks are often a world away from the pampered indoor cats of Auckland apartments. They may be semi-independent hunters who spend most of their time outdoors, only appearing for meals and the occasional cuddle. This independence makes them easier in some ways — they do not need constant attention — but harder in others, because a sitter may rarely see them and cannot easily monitor their health.

The key to successful rural cat care is establishing a feeding routine that the cat recognises and responds to. Most rural cats will appear reliably at feeding times, giving the sitter a daily health check opportunity. Leave clear instructions about feeding times, portion sizes, and any signs of illness to watch for. Ensure the sitter knows which vet to call and has authority to make emergency decisions if the cat is injured — rural cats face risks from fights, motor vehicles on rural roads, and encounters with possums or other wildlife.

If your cat is microchipped, make sure the sitter knows the chip number and registration details. If your cat goes missing, the sitter should know to contact local vets, the SPCA, and neighbours immediately rather than waiting for the cat to return. Rural properties often border other farms and lifestyle blocks, so give the sitter phone numbers for your immediate neighbours who may spot your cat on their property.

Livestock Considerations for Pet Sitters

Many lifestyle blocks in New Zealand have a small number of livestock — a few sheep, a couple of beef cattle, or some goats. While these are not technically pets, they are animals that need daily attention, and a pet sitter who is only prepared for cats and dogs may be overwhelmed by a lamb that needs bottle-feeding or a goat that has escaped again.

Be upfront with potential sitters about exactly what animals they will be caring for. A lifestyle block sitter needs to be comfortable with a wider range of animals than a typical house and pet sitter. They should know how to check water troughs, identify signs of illness in sheep and cattle, and handle basic stock tasks like moving animals between paddocks. If you have animals that require specific care — such as a lamb on milk replacer or a goat with hoof problems — make sure the sitter is confident they can manage this before you commit.

Leave detailed written instructions for each species on your property, including normal behaviour, feeding regimes, signs of illness, and emergency procedures. Include the phone number of your vet and, if possible, a neighbour who knows your stock and can help in an emergency. Remember that rural vets may have limited after-hours availability, so having a backup plan is essential.

Pest Control and Predator Management

Rural New Zealand has a significant predator problem. Stoats, ferrets, rats, possums, and feral cats are constant threats to chooks, pet rabbits, and even small dogs. A lifestyle block sitter needs to understand the predator risks in your area and the measures you have in place to manage them.

If you have traps or bait stations set, make sure the sitter knows exactly where they are, what they are catching, and what to do with catches. Many rural properties are part of community predator control programmes, and your sitter may need to check and reset trap lines daily. This is skilled work — do not assume a general pet sitter will know how to handle it. If trapping is part of the daily routine, mention this clearly in your sitter listing and during the meet-and-greet.

For properties near bush or native wildlife areas, dog control is also important. Even well-trained dogs can be tempted by kiwi, weka, or other native birds. Ensure your sitter understands local dog control rules, keeps dogs on-lead near native bush areas, and knows that attacking native wildlife can result in serious consequences under New Zealand law, including the destruction of the dog.

Fencing, Gates, and Property Security

Rural properties have fencing and gate systems that are far more complex than any urban home. Boundary fences, internal paddock divisions, stock yards, and gates that must remain closed at all times — the sitter needs to understand all of it. A gate left open on a lifestyle block can mean stock on the road, neighbour complaints, and potentially serious accidents.

Walk the sitter around the entire property before you leave. Show them every gate, explain which ones must remain closed and which can be left open. Identify any fences that are in poor repair and need watching. Point out hazards like farm effluent ponds, steep drops, or areas where stock can become trapped. This orientation is essential — do not skip it, even if the sitter has experience with rural properties. Every lifestyle block is different.

If your property has electric fences, make sure the sitter knows how the system works, where the energiser is, and how to check that fences are live. Give them a volt meter or at least a testing tool, and explain what to do if a fence fault occurs. Electric fences that are not working can result in stock wandering onto roads or into neighbour properties — a serious safety and liability issue.

Water Systems and Tank Monitoring

Most rural NZ properties rely on rainwater tanks, bore water, or a combination of both. Water supply is a critical responsibility that a lifestyle block sitter must understand. A broken pump or a leaking pipe can leave animals without water within hours, which is an animal welfare emergency.

Show your sitter where the water pump is, how to check water levels in tanks, and what to do if the pump stops working. Leave the number of a local plumber or pump specialist who can respond quickly in an emergency. If your property uses a bore, explain any quirks like pressure fluctuations or seasonal variation in supply. Ensure the sitter checks water troughs daily and knows how to refill them manually if the automatic system fails.

Finding the Right Rural Pet Sitter

Not every pet sitter is cut out for lifestyle block care. You need someone who is comfortable around a variety of animals, not just cats and dogs. They should be physically capable of handling stock, confident with rural environments, and prepared for the reality that lifestyle block sitting often involves more work than a typical house sit.

When searching for a sitter on ZiggySitters, look for profiles that mention rural experience, farming backgrounds, or comfort with multiple animal species. During the meet-and-greet, be thorough about what the role involves. Show them the chook coop, introduce them to the working dogs, walk them through the feeding routines, and make sure they understand the daily schedule. A sitter who looks uncertain about any aspect is probably not the right fit — lifestyle blocks need confident, capable care.

Creating a Lifestyle Block Care Manual

Every lifestyle block should have a written care manual that stays on the property for sitters. This manual should cover the daily routine from start to finish, feeding instructions for every animal, the location of feed stores and supplies, water system details, fencing and gate rules, emergency contacts including vet, plumber, electrician, and a trusted neighbour, and any quirks specific to your property or animals.

Think of this manual as the operating guide for your lifestyle block. The more detailed it is, the more confident your sitter will be, and the less likely things are to go wrong. Include photos of each animal with their name, a map of the property showing paddocks, gates, water points, and hazards, and a step-by-step daily checklist. Update the manual whenever routines change, and review it with each new sitter before you leave.

A good lifestyle block care manual takes an hour or two to write but saves days of stress. It means you can go on holiday knowing that whoever is looking after your property has everything they need to keep your animals safe, healthy, and happy — and that is worth every minute of preparation.
