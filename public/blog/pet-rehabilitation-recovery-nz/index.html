Pet Rehabilitation & Recovery After Surgery in New Zealand

Published: 27 July 2026 • By ZiggySitters NZ • 13 min read

The surgery is over, but the hard work is just beginning. Post-operative rehabilitation is where outcomes are won or lost. This guide covers everything NZ pet owners need to know about recovery timelines, home care, physiotherapy, pain management, and getting the right support for your pet while they heal.

Why Rehabilitation Matters

Most pet owners focus on the surgery itself and underestimate the recovery. Here is the reality: a TPLO (Tibial Plateau Leveling Osteotomy) for a torn cruciate ligament takes 8-12 weeks of structured rehabilitation. Skip that rehab, and your dog may never regain full mobility. Push too hard too fast, and you risk re-injury that requires a second surgery.

Rehabilitation is not optional — it is the second half of the treatment. The vet fixes the problem. Rehabilitation makes the fix permanent.

Key statistic: Dogs who complete a structured 12-week rehabilitation programme after TPLO surgery regain 90-95% of normal limb function. Dogs with no rehabilitation typically regain only 50-60%. The difference is not small — it is the difference between a normal life and permanent lameness.

Common Surgeries and Recovery Timelines

Every surgery has its own recovery arc. Understanding what is normal and what is a red flag helps you advocate for your pet at every stage.

TPLO (cruciate repair) 8-12 weeks 4-6 months Crate rest, controlled leash walks only

IVDD spinal surgery 6-8 weeks 3-6 months Strict crate rest, no stairs, no jumping
Fracture repair (plate/pin) 6-12 weeks 3-6 months Crate rest, physiotherapy at 4-6 weeks
Amputation (limb) 2-3 weeks 4-8 weeks Confinement, rehab for balance adaptation

These timelines are guides, not guarantees. Every pet heals at their own pace. Your vet will assess progress at post-op checkups and adjust the timeline. Never advance to the next rehabilitation phase without your vet is explicit go-ahead.

The Phases of Recovery

Phase 1: Immediate Post-Op (Days 1-3)

This is the most critical and stressful period. Your pet is coming off anaesthesia, dealing with pain, and confused about what just happened.

  • Keep them confined. A crate or small room with no furniture to jump on. No stairs. No other pets bothering them.
  • Monitor anaesthesia recovery. Some grogginess is normal for 12-24 hours. If your pet is not responsive or cannot stand after 24 hours, call your vet immediately.
  • Pain management is non-negotiable. Give all prescribed pain medication on schedule. Do not skip doses because your pet “seems fine.” They seem fine because the medication is working.
  • Watch the incision site. Mild redness at the edges is normal. Increased swelling, discharge, heat, or a bad smell means infection — call your vet.
  • Limited food and water. Offer small amounts at first. Vomiting after anaesthesia is common in the first 12 hours. If vomiting continues beyond that, contact your vet.

Phase 2: Early Healing (Days 4-14)

Your pet starts feeling better before they are healed. This is the danger zone — they want to do more than their body can handle.

  • Strict activity restriction continues. Leash-only outdoor access for toileting. No off-lead time. No playing with other pets.
  • The cone of shame exists for a reason. Licking or chewing the incision is the most common cause of surgical site infection and wound breakdown. Keep the e-collar or recovery suit on at all times unless directly supervised.
  • Begin passive range-of-motion exercises if your vet has prescribed them. Gently flex and extend the affected limb through its comfortable range. Do not push through resistance or pain.
  • Ice therapy: Apply a frozen pea bag wrapped in a tea towel to the surgical site for 10 minutes, 2-3 times daily, during the first week. This reduces swelling and pain. NZ vet clinics often provide cold packs at discharge.
  • Sutures or staples are typically removed at 10-14 days. Do not miss this appointment. Healing skin looks better from the outside than it is underneath.

Phase 3: Rehabilitation Begins (Weeks 2-6)

This is where structured rehabilitation becomes essential. Your vet will clear you to begin controlled exercise and may refer you to a veterinary physiotherapist.

  • Controlled leash walks start at 5 minutes, 2-3 times daily, on flat ground. Increase by 5 minutes per week if your vet is happy with progress.
  • Physiotherapy sessions typically happen 1-2 times weekly. A veterinary physiotherapist will use techniques like therapeutic laser, massage, passive range-of-motion, and gradually introduce active exercises.
  • Underwater treadmill is a game-changer for orthopaedic recoveries. The buoyancy reduces weight-bearing by up to 60% while allowing natural gait patterns. Available at specialist clinics in Auckland, Wellington, and Christchurch.
  • Do not allow free running or off-lead activity. A single sprint after a bird can undo weeks of careful rehabilitation. Use a long lead for outdoor toileting and short controlled walks for exercise.

Phase 4: Return to Function (Weeks 6-12+)

Gradual return to normal activity with your vet is clearance at each step.

  • Increase walk duration and terrain gradually. Flat paths first, then gentle hills, then uneven ground. Each new surface tests stability differently.
  • Introduce controlled play. Retrieve games on flat ground (no jumping). Gentle tug games. Short bursts of off-lead time in a secure, enclosed area.
  • Continue physiotherapy as recommended. Many pets benefit from 2-3 months of structured rehabilitation, not just the initial recovery phase.
  • Watch for setback signs: sudden limping, reluctance to bear weight, swelling at the surgical site, or decreased appetite. Stop activity immediately and call your vet.

Crate Rest: The Hardest Part

Crate rest is the most commonly prescribed and most commonly botched recovery requirement. A crate-rested pet is frustrated, bored, and desperate for stimulation. Here is how to survive it without losing your sanity or theirs.

Setting Up the Crate

  • Size: Large enough for your pet to stand, turn around, and lie down comfortably. For dogs, a crate 1.5 times their body length minimum.
  • Location: A central room where the family spends time. Isolation increases anxiety. Your pet needs to see and hear you.
  • Bedding: Orthopaedic memory foam or vet bed (available at NZ pet stores). Convoluted foam absorbs pressure and provides support for recovering joints.
  • Padding the sides: If your pet is a restless sleeper or risk of bumping the surgical site, line the crate walls with blankets or pool noodles ziptied to the bars.

Managing Boredom During Crate Rest

  • Kong toys stuffed with wet food and frozen — 30-60 minutes of engagement per Kong
  • Snuffle mats — hide treats in the fabric strips for nose work that does not require movement
  • Lick mats — spread with yoghurt, wet food, or NZ-made K9 Natural and freeze
  • Audio enrichment — leave the radio on, play classical music (shown to reduce canine stress), or audiobooks for company
  • Training from the crate — teach new tricks that do not require movement: touch, look, speak, shake
  • Chew toys — NZ deer antlers, nylon bones, or long-lasting natural chews for dental health and boredom relief
Crate rest is not punishment. Frame it for yourself and your pet: this is a healing cocoon, not a prison. Dogs who are crate-rested properly heal faster because they are not re-injuring themselves. Cats adapt surprisingly well to a large dog crate with a litter box, bed, and hiding spot.

Pain Management

Effective pain control is essential for healing. Pets in pain eat less, move less, and recover slower. Modern veterinary pain management in NZ uses a multi-modal approach — combining different types of medication to target pain from multiple angles.

Common Post-Op Pain Medications in NZ

Medication Type Used For Notes
Meloxicam (Metacam) NSAID Inflammation and mild-moderate pain Liquid, easy to dose; give with food
Carprofen (Rimadyl) NSAID Orthopaedic pain Chewable tablets; monitor liver/kidney function
Tramadol Opioid Moderate-severe pain Can cause sedation or constipation
Gabapentin Nerve pain Nerve-related and chronic pain Especially useful for spinal surgery recovery
Buprenorphine Opioid Post-op (first 24-72 hours) Often given by injection at the clinic
Paracetamol (Panadol) Analgesic Mild pain (dogs only) NEVER give to cats — toxic at any dose
Never give human pain medication to your pet without veterinary instruction. Ibuprofen, aspirin, and paracetamol (for cats) are toxic and can cause fatal organ damage. Only use medications prescribed by your NZ vet, at the exact dose and frequency specified.

Non-Pharmaceutical Pain Relief

  • Cold therapy: Ice packs wrapped in cloth, applied for 10 minutes, 2-3 times daily during the first week post-op. Reduces swelling and numbs the area.
  • Heat therapy: After the first week (once acute swelling has subsided), warm compresses increase blood flow and relax tight muscles around the surgical site.
  • Therapeutic laser (Cold Laser Therapy): Available at many NZ vet clinics. Reduces inflammation, accelerates tissue healing, and provides pain relief without medication side effects. Sessions are 5-15 minutes and cost $30-60 each.
  • Massage: Gentle massage around (not on) the surgical site reduces muscle tension and improves circulation. Ask your vet or physiotherapist to demonstrate safe techniques.

Veterinary Physiotherapy in NZ

New Zealand has a growing network of veterinary physiotherapists who specialise in post-surgical rehabilitation. A referral from your vet is typically required, and many work alongside specialist clinics.

What Veterinary Physiotherapy Includes

  • Manual therapy: Joint mobilisations, soft tissue massage, stretching
  • Therapeutic exercises: Sit-to-stand, weight shifting, balance board, cavaletti rails
  • Electrotherapy: TENS, neuromuscular stimulation for muscle re-education
  • Hydrotherapy: Underwater treadmill or swimming (discussed in our hydrotherapy guide)
  • Laser therapy: Photobiomodulation for tissue healing and pain relief
  • Home programme: Exercises you do between sessions — this is where the real progress happens

Finding a Veterinary Physiotherapist

Look for practitioners registered with the New Zealand Institute of Animal and Equine Physiotherapists (NZAEP) or with equivalent UK/Australian qualifications. Your vet can refer you, or search the NZAEP practitioner directory online.

Typical costs in NZ:

  • Initial consultation: $90-$150
  • Follow-up sessions: $60-$100
  • Underwater treadmill session: $50-$80
  • Therapeutic laser session: $30-$60

Some pet insurance policies in NZ cover physiotherapy. Check your policy — PD Insurance and Petplan both offer rehabilitation cover as an optional add-on or inclusion.

Wound Care at Home

Proper wound care prevents infection and dehiscence (wound breakdown), which are the two most common surgical complications in recovering pets.

Daily Wound Checks

  • Look for: Increased redness, swelling, heat, discharge (especially yellow, green, or foul-smelling), or gaps in the incision line.
  • Feel for: Excessive warmth around the incision compared to surrounding skin. A slight bump is normal (surgical glue or knots), but a growing lump is not.
  • Smell for: Any odour coming from the wound. Surgical sites should not smell.
  • Check under bandages: If your pet has a bandage, check the skin at the top and bottom edges for chafing, swelling, or moisture. Never remove a bandage without your vet is instruction.

Preventing Licking and Chewing

  • E-collar (cone): The traditional plastic cone is ugly but effective. It must extend past the nose to prevent access to the wound.
  • Recovery suits: Fabric body suits (available from NZ stores like Bark & Beyond, or online) cover the surgical site while allowing toileting. Better tolerated by most pets than cones.
  • Inflatable collars: Good for abdominal and chest wounds but less effective for paw and leg surgeries where pets can reach around the collar.
  • Bitter spray: Apply around (not on) the incision as an extra deterrent. Available at vet clinics and pet stores.
If your pet removes their e-collar or recovery suit and licks the wound, do not wait to see if it heals on its own. Contact your vet immediately. One night of licking can introduce a deep infection that adds weeks to recovery and may require a second surgery.

Pet Sitting for Recovering Pets

If you need to leave your recovering pet — for work, a wedding, or any reason — a pet sitter experienced with post-surgical care is essential. Standard pet sitting does not cover the level of attention a recovering animal requires.

What a Post-Surgical Pet Sitter Needs to Know

  • Medication schedule: Exact times, doses, and how to administer (pills hidden in food, liquid via syringe, injections if applicable).
  • Activity restrictions: Exactly what your pet can and cannot do. Leash-only outside? No stairs? Crate rest except for toileting?
  • Wound care protocol: How to check the incision, what to look for, and when to call the vet versus when to call you.
  • Emergency contacts: Your regular vet, the nearest after-hours clinic, and a friend or family member who can make decisions if you are unreachable.
  • E-collar or recovery suit rules: The sitter must understand that these stay ON at all times unless you have explicitly authorised removal.
  • Appetite and toileting expectations: Reduced appetite is normal for 24-48 hours post-op. Straining to urinate, blood in urine, or no toileting for 24+ hours requires a vet call.

What to Look for in a Recovery Pet Sitter

  • Experience with post-surgical pets — ask specifically about TPLO, IVDD, or similar recoveries
  • Confidence administering medication (pills, liquid, and ideally injections)
  • Willingness to follow your vet is exact instructions, even if your pet seems “fine”
  • Ability to transport your pet to the vet in an emergency
  • Daily photo and video updates so you can assess your pet is body language and wound
ZiggySitters tip: When searching for a sitter for a recovering pet, include “post-surgery experience” or “medical pet sitting” in your search. At the meet-and-greet, demonstrate exactly how you give medication and check the wound. A sitter who has handled a TPLO recovery before will be far more confident than one who has only done healthy pet sits.

NZ-Specific Considerations for Pet Recovery

Climate and Season

  • Summer recovery: NZ UV is extreme. If your pet is recovering in summer with a shaved surgical site, limit outdoor time to early morning and late evening. Apply vet-approved sunscreen to exposed skin. Never walk on hot pavement — if you cannot hold your hand on it for seven seconds, it is too hot for paws.
  • Winter recovery: Cold and damp NZ winters slow healing and make stiff joints worse. Provide warm bedding, avoid long periods on cold concrete, and ask your vet about joint supplements if your pet is recovering from orthopaedic surgery.
  • Rainy day toileting: Crate-rested dogs still need to go outside for toileting. Have a clear, short path from crate to grass that stays consistent. A covered area or umbrella for you makes rainy toilet breaks less miserable for both of you.

Vet Access in NZ

  • After-hours care: Major cities have 24/7 emergency vet clinics (Auckland: Emergency Vet Clinic Auckland, Wellington: After Hours Veterinary Hospital, Christchurch: Christchurch After Hours Vet). Rural areas may have limited options — ask your regular vet for the nearest after-hours facility before surgery.
  • Follow-up appointments: Most NZ vets schedule a post-op check at 3-5 days and a suture removal at 10-14 days. Do not skip these, even if the wound looks fine.
  • Referral specialists: Complex surgeries (TPLO, IVDD, FHO) are often performed at referral centres. Your pet is follow-up may be split between your regular vet and the specialist. Keep both in the loop.

Pet Insurance and Cost

Surgical costs in NZ are significant. TPLO surgery can cost $4,000-$7,000. IVDD spinal surgery can reach $8,000-$12,000. Pet insurance (if obtained before the condition develops) can cover 80-100% of surgical and rehabilitation costs. Key NZ insurers:

  • Petplan NZ: Covers surgery, hospitalisation, and physiotherapy. Lifetime cover available.
  • PD Insurance: Accident and illness cover with rehabilitation add-on options.
  • Southern Cross Pet Insurance: Comprehensive cover including specialist referrals.

If your pet is already insured, notify your insurer before surgery and keep all invoices. Most NZ insurers require pre-approval for claims over a certain threshold.

When Things Go Wrong

Recovery is not always smooth. Know when to call the vet and when to head to emergency:

Call Your Vet (Same Day)

  • Mild increase in swelling or redness at the incision site
  • Decreased appetite lasting more than 24 hours
  • Pet seems painful despite medication (panting, restlessness, guarding the area)
  • Mild discharge from the incision that is clear or slightly pink
  • Constipation lasting more than 48 hours (common with pain medication)

Go to Emergency (Immediately)

  • Incision opens or sutures come out
  • Thick, yellow/green, or foul-smelling discharge from the wound
  • Sudden inability to bear weight on the operated limb
  • Severe lethargy, non-responsiveness, or difficulty breathing
  • Vomiting that prevents keeping medication down
  • Body temperature above 39.5°C (dogs) or 39.2°C (cats)
  • Any seizure, collapse, or loss of consciousness
Trust your gut. You know your pet better than anyone. If something feels wrong, call your vet. It is always better to make a unnecessary trip than to miss a real complication.

Supporting Your Pet Emotionally

Pets do not understand why they are confined, why they hurt, or why you keep putting pills in their food. Emotional support matters for recovery as much as physical care.

  • Stay calm. Pets pick up on your anxiety. If you are stressed about the recovery, they will be too.
  • Be present. Sit with your crated pet. Read aloud, work on your laptop nearby, or just be there. Presence is comfort.
  • Celebrate small wins. First time standing unassisted? First short walk without limping? These are milestones. Acknowledge them with calm praise and a treat.
  • Watch for depression. Loss of interest in food, toys, or interaction beyond normal post-op grogginess may indicate pain or depression. Talk to your vet about adjusting the plan.
  • Keep other pets separated if needed. A boisterous housemate can re-injure a recovering pet in seconds. Use baby gates, separate rooms, or supervised introductions only.

Final Thoughts

Post-surgical rehabilitation is a marathon, not a sprint. The first two weeks are the hardest — your pet is in pain, confined, and frustrated. You are tired, worried, and managing medication schedules around the clock. It gets better. By weeks 3-4, most pets start to show their old personality again. By weeks 6-8, you will see the recovery physically in how they move.

Follow your vet is instructions exactly. Do not rush the timeline. Do not skip the boring home exercises. Do not take the cone off early because your pet looks sad. The discipline you show during recovery is the difference between a full recovery and a permanent limitation.

And if you need help — whether that is a pet sitter experienced in post-surgical care, a veterinary physiotherapist, or just someone to remind you that day 5 is always the hardest — reach out. You do not have to do this alone.
