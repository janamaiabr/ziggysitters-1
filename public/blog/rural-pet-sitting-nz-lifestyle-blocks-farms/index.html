Rural Pet Sitting on NZ Lifestyle Blocks and Farms: A Complete Guide

Published on 5 August 2026 | By ZiggySitters

Pet sitting in rural New Zealand is a different world from minding a cat in a Wellington apartment. Lifestyle blocks, farmlets, and full-scale working farms bring a unique set of animals, routines, hazards, and responsibilities that urban sitters rarely encounter. Whether you are a pet sitter considering your first rural booking or a lifestyle block owner looking for someone reliable to care for your property, this guide covers everything you need to know about rural pet sitting in New Zealand.

What Makes Rural Pet Sitting Different

A lifestyle block in New Zealand typically ranges from one to ten hectares and may include a mix of companion animals and small livestock. Dogs and cats are standard, but you might also find chickens, ducks, a few sheep, goats, alpacas, or even a kunekune pig. Working farms are a step up again, with dairy herds, beef cattle, and large flocks of sheep requiring daily attention.

The key difference is responsibility. An urban pet sitter feeds the cat and walks the dog. A rural sitter manages water troughs, checks fences, collects eggs, and watches for signs of illness in animals that cannot tell you something is wrong. The scope is broader, the stakes are higher, and the isolation is real. If something goes wrong at 2 AM on a lifestyle block an hour from the nearest vet, you need to handle it yourself until help arrives.

Rural properties also have infrastructure that needs monitoring. Pumps, septic systems, electric fences, and gas hot water systems can all malfunction. A good rural sitter is part animal carer, part handyman, and part problem solver.

Understanding NZ Lifestyle Block Animals

Chickens and Poultry

Chickens are the most common lifestyle block livestock in New Zealand. A small flock of six to twelve hens is standard on most blocks around Canterbury, Waikato, and the Bay of Plenty. Daily care involves collecting eggs, checking water and feed, and ensuring the coop is securely closed at dusk to protect against stoats, ferrets, and roaming dogs. Free-range flocks also need to be checked for signs of predatory attacks or illness.

Ducks are increasingly popular on lifestyle blocks, particularly Indian Runners and Khaki Campbells, which are excellent for pest control and egg production. Ducks need access to fresh water for bathing and are messier than chickens, so their enclosure requires more frequent cleaning. If you are sitting a property with ducks, check that their water supply is clean and functioning each morning and evening.

Sheep and Goats

Many lifestyle block owners keep a handful of sheep as lawnmowers and for occasional freezer fill. Pet sheep, particularly hand-reared orphans called pet lambs, can be surprisingly affectionate and will follow you around the paddock. Wethers and ewes need fresh water, shade in summer, and shelter from wind and rain. In winter, supplementary feed such as hay or sheep nuts may be required if pasture is sparse.

Goats are mischievous escape artists and will test every fence on the property. If you are sitting a block with goats, check fences daily. Goats also need shelter from rain, as they lack the waterproof fleece of sheep, and they are prone to foot problems in muddy conditions. Dairy goats such as Saanen or Toggenburg need milking once or twice a day, which the owner should train you for before departure.

Alpacas and Llamas

Alpacas have become a fixture on New Zealand lifestyle blocks, prized for their fleece and their gentle, curious nature. They are relatively low maintenance but do require shearing annually, toe nail trimming, and vaccination against clostridial diseases. As a sitter, your main responsibilities are fresh water, hay if pasture is limited, and monitoring for signs of distress or illness. Alpacas are herd animals and should never be kept in isolation, so check that all members of the group are present and behaving normally.

Kunekune Pigs

Kunekune pigs are a uniquely New Zealand breed and a popular lifestyle block addition. They are friendly, intelligent, and surprisingly clean. Care involves feeding once or twice daily with pig nuts and fresh vegetables, providing clean water, and ensuring their shelter is dry and draught-free. Kunekune pigs are grazers and can become overweight easily, so portion control matters. They also need wallow areas in summer to cool down.

Rural Hazards That Urban Sitters Underestimate

Water Systems

Most rural New Zealand properties rely on rainwater tanks, bore water, or a combination of both. If the pump fails, there is no water for animals, toilets, or showers. Before the owner leaves, make sure you know where the pump switch is, how to reset it, and who to call for emergency repairs. Check that the water troughs in paddocks are flowing and not frozen or blocked. A dry trough in summer can kill livestock within hours.

Electric Fences

Electric fences keep livestock in and predators out, but they can also trip or short. Check the fence energiser daily. If you hear a constant clicking or see the voltage drop, something is shorting the fence, usually a branch, tall grass, or a broken insulator. Carry a small voltmeter and know where the earth stakes are. If the voltage drops below 3000 volts, stock can push through.

Fires and Weather

Rural New Zealand properties face real fire risk, particularly in Canterbury, Marlborough, and Central Otago during dry summers. Know where the fire extinguishers are, ensure access routes are clear, and have a plan for moving animals if you need to evacuate. In winter, heavy snow can collapse greenhouse tunnels, block access roads, and freeze water lines. Ask the owner what their plan is for extreme weather events.

Stock Escapes

If a gate is left open or a fence fails, livestock on a lifestyle block will wander onto the road. This is dangerous for drivers and the animals. Walk the boundary fences on your first day and report any damage immediately. Know which paddock gates should be open for stock rotation and which must remain closed. If animals do escape, the owner should leave contact numbers for neighbours who can help with mustering.

The Rural Pet Sitting Checklist

Before taking on a rural pet sitting assignment, make sure you have clear instructions on all of the following:

  • Daily routine: What time are animals fed, watered, and checked? What order do tasks happen in?
  • Feed quantities and locations: How much hay, grain, or pellets does each animal group receive? Where is the feed stored?
  • Water systems: Where is the pump? How do you reset it? What is the emergency plumber number?
  • Fence checks: Which fences need daily inspection? Where are spare fence tools?
  • Veterinary contacts: Not just the local vet, but after-hours emergency numbers and any large-animal vets in the area.
  • Neighbour contacts: Who can help in an emergency? Who has a spare key?
  • Machinery: What needs to be run or maintained while the owner is away? Ride-on mower, tractor, quad bike?
  • Rubbish and recycling: What day is collection? Where are the bins?
  • Septic system: Does it need attention? Where is the tank and access point?
  • Emergency veterinary budget: How much can you spend before needing to call the owner for approval?

What Rural Pet Sitters Should Charge

Rural pet sitting commands a premium over urban sitting because of the additional responsibilities and isolation. In New Zealand, typical rates in 2026 are:

  • Lifestyle block with dogs, cats, and chickens: $80 to $120 per night
  • Lifestyle block with small livestock (sheep, goats, alpacas): $100 to $150 per night
  • Working farm with livestock requiring twice-daily checks: $150 to $250 per night
  • Properties requiring milking or intensive animal care: $200 to $300 per night

These rates reflect the fact that rural sitters are effectively working a job, not just staying in a house. Morning and evening livestock checks alone can take an hour each. Add in feeding, watering, egg collection, fence checks, and cleaning, and a rural sitting assignment is a significant daily commitment.

If you are a property owner, remember that paying a fair rate attracts experienced sitters who will actually look after your animals and property. The cheapest sitter is rarely the best value when your animals' welfare is at stake.

Finding the Right Rural Pet Sitter

Not every pet sitter is suited to rural assignments. When looking for someone to care for your lifestyle block or farm, prioritise these qualities:

  • Experience with livestock: Even basic experience with chickens, sheep, or goats makes a significant difference. Ask about specific scenarios, such as what they would do if a chicken was attacked or a sheep was limping.
  • Comfort with isolation: Rural blocks can be lonely, especially in winter when the sun sets at 5 PM and the nearest shop is a 20-minute drive. Sitters who need city conveniences will struggle.
  • Physical fitness: Carrying hay bales, walking paddocks in mud, and handling animals requires reasonable fitness. This is not a sitting role in the traditional sense.
  • Problem-solving ability: Things break on rural properties. A good sitter can think on their feet and handle minor repairs without panicking.
  • Reliable transport: A vehicle is essential on a lifestyle block. Sitters need to be able to reach the vet, the feed store, and civilisation if required.

Seasonal Considerations

Spring (September to November)

Spring is lambing and calving season. If you are sitting a property with breeding stock, be prepared for night checks during late pregnancies and the possibility of assisting with difficult births. The owner should provide clear instructions and emergency contacts for a vet who can attend a calving or lambing. Spring weather in New Zealand is unpredictable, so check that all shelters are intact and that newborn animals are warm and dry.

Summer (December to February)

Summer brings heat, flies, and the risk of flystrike in sheep. Check sheep daily for signs of flystrike, particularly around the tail and breech. Ensure all animals have access to shade and clean water. Alpacas and goats need shearing or at least shearing preparation in early summer. Rural properties in dry regions like Canterbury and Hawke's Bay may face water restrictions, so confirm the owner's preferences for garden watering versus stock water priority.

Autumn (March to May)

Autumn is a relatively calm period for rural pet sitting. The main tasks are regular feeding, checking water systems before winter, and ensuring shelters are in good repair before the cold sets in. It is also the time when many lifestyle block owners wean lambs and calves, so check whether any dietary changes are planned.

Winter (June to August)

Winter is the most demanding season for rural sitters. Frost can freeze water troughs overnight, requiring you to break ice each morning and sometimes run a heat lamp or trough heater. Supplementary feeding increases as pasture declines. Mud becomes a real issue on Canterbury and Waikato clay soils, making paddock access difficult. Check that gate latches work in freezing conditions, as metal can seize. Dogs on rural properties may need coats for early morning walks, and older animals may need arthritis medication adjusted.

Legal Responsibilities for Rural Pet Sitters

New Zealand's Animal Welfare Act 1999 applies to anyone in charge of an animal, including pet sitters. This means you have a legal obligation to meet the physical, health, and behavioural needs of every animal under your care, whether it is a cat, a chicken, or a cow. Failure to provide adequate food, water, shelter, or veterinary care is an offence.

For lifestyle blocks and farms, this translates to practical obligations:

  • All animals must have access to clean, fresh water at all times
  • Sick or injured animals must receive prompt veterinary attention
  • Shelter must be available for all animals during extreme weather
  • Handling must be done in a way that minimises pain and distress
  • Any surgical procedure, such as tail docking or castration, must be performed by a veterinarian or trained person

As a sitter, you should also be aware of your liability for stock on the road. If animals in your care escape and cause a traffic accident, there are legal and insurance implications. Ensure fences are secure and gates are closed at all times.

Making Rural Pet Sitting Work

Rural pet sitting can be incredibly rewarding. You get to live in beautiful surroundings, care for fascinating animals, and experience a side of New Zealand life that most visitors never see. But it demands more preparation, more vigilance, and more common sense than urban sitting assignments.

If you are a lifestyle block owner, invest time in a thorough handover before you leave. Walk the sitter through every task, introduce them to every animal, and make sure they can reach you or a knowledgeable neighbour in an emergency. The best rural sitters are those who feel confident and prepared, not those who are left to figure things out on their own.

If you are a sitter, be honest about your experience level. Do not take on a farm sitting assignment with dairy cattle if you have never handled livestock. Start with smaller lifestyle blocks, build your skills, and work your way up to more complex assignments.
