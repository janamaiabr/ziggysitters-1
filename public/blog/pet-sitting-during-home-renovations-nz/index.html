Pet Sitting During Home Renovations in NZ: Keeping Pets Safe and Stress-Free

Home renovations are stressful enough for the humans living through them. For your pets, they can be downright traumatic. Dust, noise, strangers in the house, toxic fumes, open doors, and disrupted routines all combine to turn a familiar home into a confusing, sometimes dangerous environment. If you are planning renovations on your New Zealand home and you have pets, this guide will help you decide whether to keep them at home, relocate them temporarily, or bring in a pet sitter to bridge the gap.

Why Renovations Are Hard on Pets

Pets thrive on predictability. Their feeding times, walk schedules, sleeping spots, and territory boundaries all form a routine they depend on for a sense of safety. Renovations destroy that routine in ways both obvious and subtle:

  • Noise: Hammering, drilling, sawing, and machinery produce sounds at volumes that can cause hearing stress and anxiety, especially in sensitive breeds.
  • Dust and fumes: Plaster dust, sawdust, paint fumes, solvent vapours, and adhesive off-gassing are all respiratory irritants for animals who spend most of their time at floor level where particulates concentrate.
  • Strangers: Tradespeople coming and going means unfamiliar smells, voices, and movements throughout the day. For territorial or anxious pets, this is deeply unsettling.
  • Escape routes: Open walls, missing doors, and tradespeople propping doors open create escape opportunities that did not exist before.
  • Physical hazards: Loose nails, screws, broken glass, exposed wiring, and wet paint or sealant are all injury risks for curious animals.
  • Routine disruption: Feeding, walking, and sleeping schedules get shifted or skipped entirely. For pets that depend on routine, this alone can trigger behavioural issues.

Option 1: Keeping Your Pet at Home

For low-to-moderate renovations, keeping your pet at home is feasible with careful planning.

Create a Safe Room

Designate one room as far from the renovation as possible. This room should have:

  • A door that closes firmly, not a temporary barrier
  • Fresh water and all familiar bedding, toys, and blankets
  • Natural light and ventilation
  • A piece of your clothing so your scent is present
  • A white noise machine or radio playing at low volume to mask construction sounds
  • A window that can be opened if paint fumes or dust travel through the house

Put a sign on the door: “Pet inside — please do not open.” Tradespeople are generally cooperative when they know an animal is behind a closed door, but they will not know unless you tell them.

Manage Air Quality

  • Run a HEPA air purifier in the safe room during and after work hours
  • Keep windows open on the renovation side when tradespeople leave, weather permitting
  • Avoid sanding or cutting inside when the pet is home if possible
  • Choose low-VOC paints and water-based sealants where you can
  • Wipe down surfaces daily to remove settled dust
  • Watch for coughing, sneezing, or watery eyes in your pet — signs of respiratory irritation

Secure the Perimeter

  • All exterior doors and gates close and latch properly
  • Temporary fencing is in place if boundary fences are being removed or modified
  • Tradespeople understand the “close every door behind you” rule
  • Your pet is microchipped and wearing a collar with up-to-date contact details

Option 2: Hiring a Pet Sitter During Renovations

For moderate-to-high impact renovations, a pet sitter is often the best solution:

  • Normal home environment: The sitter’s home or yours after hours is more comfortable than a kennel.
  • Routine maintained: A good sitter follows feeding, walking, and play schedules to the letter.
  • Someone present: A house-sitting pet sitter can stay overnight, providing companionship and security.
  • Flexibility: Pet sitters adapt as renovation timelines shift, which they always do.

How to Brief a Pet Sitter for a Renovation Sit

A renovation sit is not a standard sit. Brief your sitter on:

  1. Work schedule: What hours will tradespeople be on-site? Which days?
  2. Restricted areas: Which rooms or zones are off-limits?
  3. Hazard list: Any exposed wiring, open walls, wet paint, or toxic materials.
  4. Emergency contacts: Builder or project manager phone number, plus your vet.
  5. Dust and noise plan: Where does the pet go during work hours?
  6. Access rules: Who has keys or alarm codes?
  7. Clean-up expectations: Wipe paws after walks? Clean construction dust?

Option 3: Temporary Relocation

For full-scale renovations where no part of the house is safe or comfortable, temporary relocation is the kindest choice:

  • Pet sitter boarding: Your pet stays at a sitter’s home. More comfortable than a kennel, with personalised attention. Book early.
  • Boarding kennel or cattery: Suitable for pets that tolerate boarding well. NZ kennels range from $35 to $65 per night for dogs.
  • Family or friends: Free, but only works if the home is genuinely pet-safe and the people are reliable.
  • Pet-friendly short-term rental: Keeps you and your pet together during the renovation period.
Book your pet sitter early. Renovation timelines almost always run over. Add at least one extra week to your estimated completion date. It is far easier to cancel unused days than to find a sitter at the last minute.

Preparing Your Pet Before Renovations Start

Start these steps at least two weeks before work begins:

For Dogs

  • Desensitise to noise: Play recordings of construction sounds at low volume during meals or play. Gradually increase volume over several days.
  • Practise safe-room stays: Close your dog in the designated safe room for short periods with a stuffed Kong or chew toy. Build up duration gradually.
  • Update microchip and ID: Ensure your dog’s microchip details are current in the NZ Companion Animal Register.
  • Stock up on enrichment: Puzzle feeders, long-lasting chews, frozen Kongs, and snuffle mats keep dogs occupied when outdoor exercise is limited.
  • Vet check: If your dog has existing anxiety, talk to your vet about short-term calming supplements or medication.

For Cats

  • Set up vertical escape routes: Cat shelves, a cleared wardrobe top, or a cat tree give them somewhere to retreat above the chaos.
  • Use Feliway diffusers: Plug in pheromone diffusers in the safe room at least a week before renovations start.
  • Keep the litter tray consistent: Do not move it to the renovation zone. Disruption can cause inappropriate toileting.
  • Provide hiding spots: Cardboard boxes with blankets, cat tunnels, and covered beds give anxious cats somewhere to burrow.

During the Renovation: Daily Checklist

If your pet is staying at home, run through this checklist every day:

  • Check that the safe room is clean, well-ventilated, and free of construction dust
  • Refresh water and food bowls
  • Wipe your pet’s paws after outdoor walks (construction debris tracks in)
  • Remove any nails, screws, or debris that have migrated into pet areas
  • Confirm all exterior doors and gates are closed and latched
  • Spend at least 20 minutes of focused attention with your pet outside the renovation zone
  • Watch for signs of stress: excessive panting, hiding, loss of appetite, unusual vocalisation
  • Verify no open paint cans, solvent containers, or sharp tools are within pet reach

Common NZ Renovation Hazards for Pets

  • Asbestos: Pre-1990 homes commonly contain asbestos in soffit linings, external cladding, roofing, and vinyl floor tiles. Professional removal is mandatory; pets must be off-site.
  • Lead paint: Homes built before 1970 may have lead-based paint. Sanding creates dust that is toxic to pets who walk on floors and then lick their paws.
  • Borer dust: Common in older NZ timber frames. Can cause respiratory irritation in sensitive animals.
  • Wet NZ conditions: Winter renovations mean mud, damp, and slippery surfaces. Wipe paws after every walk.
  • Native wildlife: If fencing is down, dogs may encounter kiwi, weka, or other native birds. Keep dogs on-lead near bush areas.
If your pet ingests paint, solvent, or any construction material, contact the NZ Animal Poisons Centre on 0800 869 738 immediately. Do not wait for symptoms to appear.

After the Renovation: Reintroducing Your Pet

When the renovation is finished, do not just drop your pet back into the house and expect normality. The house will look different, smell different, and possibly have new layouts that confuse your pet. Take a gradual approach:

  1. Do a safety sweep first. Walk through every room before bringing your pet home. Check for left-behind nails, screws, paint splashes, open chemical containers, and unsecured areas. Clean all floors thoroughly.
  2. Let your pet explore on their terms. Bring them inside on-lead initially. Let them sniff and investigate at their own pace. Do not force them into new rooms.
  3. Re-establish routine immediately. Feed at the normal time. Walk at the normal time. Go to bed at the normal time. Familiar routines in a changed space help pets adjust faster.
  4. Replace old scent markers. If bedding, blankets, or scratching posts were removed or replaced, reintroduce familiar-smelling items. Your scent is the most powerful calming signal for your pet.
  5. Watch for behavioural changes. Some cats may urine-mark new surfaces. Some dogs may bark at unfamiliar layouts or new appliances. These usually resolve within a few days as the pet re-maps their territory. If problems persist beyond a week, consult your vet.

Key Takeaways

  • Assess honestly. Minor renovations can be managed at home with a safe room. Major renovations require temporary relocation.
  • Asbestos means evacuate. If your pre-1990 home has asbestos removal scheduled, your pet must be off-site. No exceptions.
  • A pet sitter is often the best middle ground. More personal than a kennel, more flexible than boarding, and your pet gets a real home environment.
  • Book early and add buffer time. Renovations always take longer than expected. Add at least a week to your sitter booking.
  • Brief your sitter thoroughly. A renovation sit needs more detail than a standard sit: work schedules, hazard lists, emergency contacts, and access rules.
  • Desensitise before work starts. Two weeks of noise exposure at low volume can prevent weeks of anxiety later.
  • Reintroduce gradually after the renovation. The house will feel like a new place to your pet. Give them time to re-map their territory.
