Saying Goodbye: End-of-Life Care & Euthanasia Guide for New Zealand Pet Owners

Saying goodbye to a beloved pet is one of the hardest experiences any pet owner faces. Whether the decision comes after a long illness, a sudden decline, or the natural progression of old age, knowing what to expect and what options are available can bring comfort during an incredibly difficult time. This guide provides honest, compassionate information about end-of-life care for pets in New Zealand, including how to assess quality of life, what the euthanasia process involves, at-home options, costs, and where to find grief support.

Knowing When It Is Time

One of the most agonising questions pet owners face is whether it is the right time. There is rarely a single clear moment. Instead, most veterinarians encourage you to consider your pet's overall quality of life rather than waiting for one definitive sign. The following framework can help guide your thinking.

The Quality of Life Scale

Dr Alice Villalobos, a veterinary oncologist, developed the HHHHHMM Quality of Life Scale, which many New Zealand vets recommend. Each category is scored from 0 (very poor) to 10 (excellent):

  • Hurt - Is pain being adequately managed? Can medication keep your pet comfortable?
  • Hunger - Is your pet eating enough to maintain weight? Can they be hand-fed or given appetite stimulants?
  • Hydration - Is your pet drinking enough? Can subcutaneous fluids help?
  • Hygiene - Can your pet be kept clean and free from sores, especially if incontinent?
  • Happiness - Does your pet still show interest in things they once enjoyed? Can they still experience joy?
  • Mobility - Can your pet move around enough to be comfortable? Are assistive devices like slings or ramps helping?
  • More Good Days Than Bad - Over the course of a week, are most days still enjoyable for your pet, or are the bad days outnumbering the good?

A total score above 35 generally suggests an acceptable quality of life. Below that, it may be time to have an honest conversation with your vet. However, numbers alone do not make the decision. You know your pet better than anyone, and your observations about their daily experience matter as much as any scale.

Signs It May Be Time

Beyond any scoring system, certain signs often indicate that a pet is suffering more than they are enjoying life:

  • Chronic pain that cannot be adequately managed with medication
  • Inability to eat or drink despite medical support
  • Severe breathing difficulties that cause constant distress
  • Loss of interest in all previously enjoyed activities and interactions
  • Incontinence that leads to persistent sores or infections
  • Confusion or anxiety that medication cannot ease, particularly in senior pets with cognitive decline
  • Collapse or inability to stand without assistance

If you are seeing several of these signs simultaneously, it is worth scheduling a consultation with your veterinarian to discuss prognosis, palliative options, and whether euthanasia may be the kindest choice.

The Euthanasia Process: What to Expect

Understanding what happens during euthanasia can reduce fear and help you make decisions about how and where you want to say goodbye.

At the Veterinary Clinic

Most euthanasia procedures in New Zealand follow a similar process:

  1. Sedation or pre-medication - Your pet is given a sedative injection, either under the skin or into a muscle. This helps them relax and drift into a deep sleep. Some clinics offer the option to skip this step, but most vets recommend it so your pet is fully asleep and pain-free before the final injection.
  2. The final injection - Once your pet is deeply sedated, an overdose of anaesthetic (usually pentobarbital) is administered, typically through an intravenous catheter. This stops the heart within seconds. Your pet will not feel pain during this process.
  3. Confirmation - The vet will use a stethoscope to confirm your pet's heart has stopped. In some cases, reflexive muscle twitches or a final breath may occur after death. These are normal and not a sign of suffering.

The entire process usually takes 10 to 20 minutes from start to finish. You can choose to be present for all, part, or none of the procedure. There is no wrong choice.

At-Home Euthanasia in New Zealand

An increasing number of New Zealand veterinarians offer at-home euthanasia, allowing your pet to pass in familiar surroundings without the stress of a car ride or clinical environment. Services such as Forever Loved, Pawssum, and several independent mobile vets provide at-home end-of-life care across major NZ cities and many rural areas.

Benefits of at-home euthanasia include:

  • Your pet stays in their own comfortable space
  • Other household pets can be present, which some believe helps them understand and grieve
  • No stressful car ride or waiting room
  • More private, unhurried time with your pet

At-home services typically cost more than clinic-based euthanasia due to travel and time, but many pet owners find the peace of mind worth the additional cost.

Aftercare Options

Cremation

Cremation is the most common aftercare option in New Zealand. There are two types:

  • Communal cremation - Your pet is cremated alongside other animals. Ashes are scattered at the crematorium's memorial garden and not returned to you. This is the most affordable option.
  • Individual cremation - Your pet is cremated individually and their ashes are returned to you in an urn, scatter tube, or keepsake of your choice. Many New Zealand cremation services offer memorial products including jewellery that holds a small portion of ashes.

Most veterinary clinics in New Zealand work with reputable pet cremation services and can arrange everything on your behalf. You can also contact cremation services directly.

Home Burial

New Zealand law permits home burial of pets on private property, provided you own the land or have the property owner's permission. If you choose home burial:

  • Dig the grave at least one metre deep to prevent disturbance by other animals
  • Choose a location away from waterways and septic systems
  • Consider whether you might move house in the future
  • Wrap your pet in a natural, biodegradable material

Check your local council regulations, as some areas have specific restrictions on home burials.

Alternative Memorial Options

Beyond cremation and burial, some New Zealand companies offer:

  • Aquamation (water cremation) - An eco-friendly alkaline hydrolysis process that uses water rather than flame. Available in some NZ regions.
  • Memorial jewellery - Pendants, rings, or bracelets that hold ashes, fur, or a photo.
  • Planting a memorial tree - Several NZ nurseries offer memorial tree packages, including native species.
  • Paw print keepsakes - Clay or ink paw prints that many vets can create for you at the time of euthanasia.

Talking to Children About Pet Loss

If you have children, navigating their grief alongside your own can be challenging. Child psychologists and grief counsellors offer these recommendations:

  • Be honest - Use clear, age-appropriate language. Avoid euphemisms like "put to sleep" that can confuse young children.
  • Let them say goodbye - If possible, give children the opportunity to see the pet before or after death, and to participate in a goodbye ritual.
  • Validate their feelings - Let children know it is normal to feel sad, angry, or confused. Do not minimise their grief.
  • Create a memorial - Drawing pictures, writing letters, planting a tree, or making a photo book can help children process loss.
  • Maintain routines - Consistency helps children feel safe during a time of emotional upheaval.

Grief Support Resources in New Zealand

Grief over pet loss is real and valid. The bond between humans and their animals is profound, and losing that connection deserves the same respect as any other loss.

Pet-Specific Grief Support

  • Pet Loss Support NZ - Online resources and peer support for grieving pet owners
  • NZ Companion Animal Council - Information and resources on pet loss
  • Your veterinary clinic - Many NZ vet clinics offer bereavement support or can refer you to counsellors who specialise in pet loss

Professional Counselling

  • Lifeline NZ - 0800 543 354 (free, confidential 24/7 support)
  • 1737 - Text or call 1737 for free mental health support
  • Grief Centre NZ - Counselling and support groups (general grief, but pet-inclusive)

Online Communities

Several New Zealand and international online communities provide peer support for pet loss, including Facebook groups and forums. Connecting with others who have experienced similar loss can be remarkably healing.

Making the Decision Together

If you share your pet with a partner, family member, or flatmate, the decision about when and how to say goodbye should ideally be made together. Some practical tips:

  • Schedule a vet consultation where all key decision-makers can be present or on the phone
  • Discuss your pet's quality of life honestly, using the HHHHHMM scale as a starting point
  • Agree on a threshold or timeline that honours your pet's dignity
  • Consider creating a "bucket list" of final experiences for your pet
  • If opinions differ, your vet can provide an objective medical perspective

What Pet Sitters Can Do

If your pet is receiving palliative care or you are managing a terminal condition, a compassionate pet sitter can play an important role. Professional pet sitters experienced with end-of-life care can:

  • Administer medication on schedule when you are at work
  • Provide companionship and comfort during the day
  • Monitor for changes in condition and alert you or your vet
  • Help maintain your pet's hygiene, especially if they are incontinent
  • Offer respite so you can rest, knowing your pet is not alone

When booking a sitter for an end-of-life situation, be transparent about your pet's condition. Many experienced sitters have supported families through this process and understand the sensitivity required.

Planning Ahead

While no one wants to think about losing their pet, planning ahead can reduce stress during an already difficult time. Consider:

  • Discussing end-of-life preferences with your vet before you are facing an emergency
  • Learning about at-home euthanasia options in your area before you need them
  • Deciding on aftercare preferences (cremation, burial, memorial) in advance
  • Talking to your family about wishes and expectations
  • Checking whether your pet insurance covers end-of-life costs
  • Keeping your vet's after-hours emergency number accessible

Conclusion

Losing a pet is a profound grief, but it is also the final act of love we can offer. The decision to end suffering is never easy, but it is one of the most compassionate choices a pet owner can make. New Zealand has caring veterinarians, at-home services, and grief support resources to help you through every step of this journey.

If you are facing this decision now, reach out to your veterinarian. They can help you assess your pet's quality of life honestly and without judgment.
