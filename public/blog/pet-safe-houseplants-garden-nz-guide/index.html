Pet-Safe Houseplants & Garden Plants in New Zealand: The Complete Guide

Published 28 July 2026 · 12 min read

You love your indoor jungle. Your cat loves it too, which is exactly the problem. That peace lily on the bookshelf? Toxic. The lily you brought home from the florist? Potentially fatal. The aloe vera on the kitchen windowsill? Harmful to dogs. New Zealand homes are full of plants that look innocent and are anything but when your pet decides to take a bite.

This guide covers every plant you need to worry about, every plant you can safely keep, and how to create a home and garden that looks good without landing you at the emergency vet. Because the prettiest room in your house should not be the most dangerous one for your pet.

Why Plant Toxicity Matters for NZ Pets

New Zealand has one of the highest rates of pet ownership in the world. Over 64% of Kiwi households have at least one pet, and most of those pets have access to both indoor and outdoor spaces. That means your cat is rubbing against your Monstera, your dog is sniffing the daffodil bulbs you just planted, and both of them are one curious chew away from a vet bill.

The reality is ugly. The New Zealand National Poisons Centre receives thousands of calls each year about plant exposures in pets. Cats are particularly vulnerable because they are obligate groomers: if pollen lands on their fur, they lick it off. A cat does not need to eat a lily to die from one. Walking past it, getting pollen on its coat, and grooming is enough.

Critical warning for cat owners: All species of the genus Lilium (true lilies, including Easter lilies, tiger lilies, Asiatic lilies, and Oriental lilies) and Hemerocallis (daylilies) are extremely toxic to cats. Even tiny amounts of pollen, leaves, or water from the vase can cause acute kidney failure. If you have cats, do not bring lilies into your home. Period.

Toxic Houseplants to Remove

These are common indoor plants sold in New Zealand garden centres that are toxic to pets. If you have pets, these should not be in your home.

Highly toxic (remove immediately)

  • Peace lily (Spathiphyllum) — Oral irritation, vomiting, difficulty swallowing. One of the most common houseplants in NZ and one of the most common poisoning calls.
  • Devil’s ivy / Pothos (Epipremnum aureum) — Oral irritation, swelling, vomiting. Extremely common in NZ offices and homes.
  • Philodendron (all species) — Similar to peace lily. Oral pain, swelling, vomiting. Includes the popular Philodendron bipinnatifidum (split-leaf).
  • Dieffenbachia (dumb cane) — Intense oral burning, swelling that can block airways. Named because it can make you literally dumb (unable to speak).
  • Monstera deliciosa — Oral irritation, vomiting. The trendy plant every NZ interior design account features. Beautiful. Toxic.
  • Aloe vera — Vomiting, diarrhoea, lethargy in dogs and cats. Great for human burns, bad for pet stomachs.
  • Snake plant (Sansevieria) — Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea. Low care for you, moderate risk for pets.
  • ZZ plant (Zamioculcas zamiifolia) &mdash> Vomiting, diarrhoea. Another popular low-maintenance choice that is not pet-safe.
  • Dragon tree (Dracaena marginata) — Vomiting (sometimes with blood), drooling, dilated pupils in cats.
  • Jade plant (Crassula ovata) — Vomiting, depression, incoordination. Popular succulent, toxic to both cats and dogs.
  • Euphorbia (all species) — Includes crown of thorns, pencil cactus, and poinsettia. Milky sap causes severe skin and eye irritation, vomiting, and diarrhoea.
  • Cyclamen — The tubers are highly toxic. Vomiting, seizures, and heart abnormalities. Often sold as winter indoor plants.

Moderately toxic (keep out of reach or remove)

  • Rubber plant (Ficus elastica) — Oral irritation, vomiting. Mild to moderate.
  • Weeping fig (Ficus benjamina) — Skin irritation, oral irritation. Common indoor tree.
  • English ivy (Hedera helix) — Vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea. Often used in hanging baskets indoors.
  • Amaryllis — Vomiting, depression, tremors. Bulbs are the most toxic part.
  • Daffodil bulbs — Vomiting, diarrhoea, cardiac arrhythmias. The bulbs are the most toxic part; the flowers less so, but still harmful.

Toxic Garden Plants Common in NZ

New Zealand gardens are full of plants that can harm pets. Some are obvious, others are surprising. Here are the ones most likely to cause problems.

Highly toxic garden plants

  • Oleander — Grows throughout NZ, especially in warmer regions. Every part is toxic. Do not plant it if you have pets.
  • Foxglove — Naturalised throughout NZ. The leaves and flowers contain digitalis, which affects the heart.
  • Castor oil plant — Grown as an ornamental in warmer NZ regions. Seeds contain ricin, one of the most toxic natural substances known.
  • Rhododendron and Azalea — Common in NZ gardens, especially in cooler regions. All parts are toxic. Symptoms include vomiting, drooling, and potentially fatal heart arrhythmias.
  • Daffodil and narcissus bulbs — Dogs dig up and eat bulbs. The bulbs contain lycorine, which causes severe vomiting and can cause cardiac arrhythmias.
  • Tulip bulbs — Similar to daffodils. The bulbs are the most toxic part.
  • Hydrangea — Contains cyanogenic glycosides. Can cause vomiting, depression, and in large amounts, cyanide poisoning.
  • Cyclamen — Often planted in shady garden spots. The tubers are the most toxic part.
  • Autumn crocus (Colchicum autumnale) — Not the spring crocus. This one causes severe vomiting, organ damage, and can be fatal.
  • Yew (Taxus baccata) — Common hedging plant in NZ. All parts except the red aril are highly toxic. Sudden death is possible with no warning symptoms.

Garden plants that are moderately toxic

  • Chrysanthemum — Vomiting, diarrhoea, skin irritation. The pyrethrins that make it toxic to insects also affect mammals in larger doses.
  • Begonia — The tubers are the most toxic part. Oral irritation and vomiting.
  • Tomato plants — The leaves and stems contain solanine. Ripe fruit is safe, but the green parts are not.
  • Grape vines — Grapes and raisins can cause kidney failure in dogs. The mechanism is still not fully understood.
  • Onion and garlic — Damages red blood cells in cats and dogs. A small amount of onion powder can cause anaemia.
  • Avocado — Persin causes vomiting and diarrhoea in dogs. The pit is a choking hazard.

NZ Native Plants and Pet Safety

New Zealand native plants are generally less toxic than imported ornamentals, but there are important exceptions.

Deadly native: Karaka (Corynocarpus laevigatus) produces large orange berries that are extremely toxic to dogs. The kernels contain karakin, which causes convulsions and death. Karaka trees are common in North Island coastal areas and are widely planted as street trees in Auckland. If you walk your dog near karaka trees in autumn, keep them on the lead and prevent them from eating fallen fruit.
Deadly native: Tutu (Coriaria species) grows in scrub and along stream banks throughout NZ. All parts are toxic. Symptoms include vomiting, convulsions, and respiratory failure. Tutu has caused deaths in livestock and has been responsible for honey poisoning in humans.

Safe NZ native plants for gardens with pets

  • Ponga (tree fern, Cyathea dealbata) — Not toxic, but the fronds have sharp edges. Physical hazard, not a poison.
  • Harakeke (flax, Phormium tenax) — Non-toxic. Great for NZ gardens. Birds love the nectar.
  • Kowhai (Sophora species) — The seeds are toxic if eaten in quantity, but the tree itself is fine. Low risk in practice because dogs rarely eat kowhai seeds.
  • Pittosporum species — Generally non-toxic. Common hedging plants.
  • Hebe species — Non-toxic. Excellent garden shrubs with long flowering periods.
  • Coprosma species — Non-toxic. Widely used in NZ hedging.
  • Puka (Meryta sinclairii) — Non-toxic. Dramatic foliage for subtropical gardens.
  • Renga renga lily (Arthropodium cirratum) — Non-toxic. Beautiful shade-tolerant plant.

Safe Garden Plants for Pet-Friendly Outdoor Spaces

Creating a pet-safe garden does not mean sacrificing beauty. Here are plants that thrive in New Zealand conditions and are safe for curious noses.

Pet-safe flowering plants

  • Roses (Rosa species) — Thorns are a physical hazard but the plant is non-toxic. Deadhead regularly to encourage blooming.
  • Sunflowers (Helianthus annuus) — Non-toxic, dramatic, and easy from seed. Dogs sometimes eat the seeds, which is fine.
  • Zinnias — Non-toxic, long-blooming annuals. Great for cutting gardens.
  • Marigolds (Tagetes) — Non-toxic and may even repel some garden pests.
  • Snapdragons (Antirrhinum) — Non-toxic, fun for kids and safe for pets.
  • Cornflower (Centaurea cyanus) — Non-toxic, easy, and great for pollinators.
  • Nasturtium (Tropaeolum majus) — Non-toxic and edible for humans too. Great trailing plant.
  • Camellia species — Non-toxic. Stunning winter-spring flowering shrubs that thrive in NZ conditions.
  • Hellebore (Helleborus) — CAUTION: Some sources list hellebores as toxic. However, they are rarely eaten by pets because they taste extremely bitter. Low practical risk but plant with awareness.

Pet-safe herbs and edibles

  • Basil, rosemary, thyme, oregano, parsley, cilantro — All safe. Basil is a cat favourite.
  • Mint (Mentha) — Safe, but invasive. Plant in pots to contain it.
  • Strawberries — Safe and dogs love them. A few strawberries a day is a healthy treat.
  • Blueberries — Safe. The bushes are non-toxic and the fruit is a healthy dog treat.
Garden herbs to avoid: Onions, garlic, chives, and leeks are all toxic to cats and dogs. If you grow them, fence them off or grow them in raised beds your pet cannot reach. Also avoid growing comfrey, which contains pyrrolizidine alkaloids that damage the liver.

Cat Grass and Safe Indoor Alternatives

Cats eat plants. It is not a deficiency, it is normal behaviour. The trick is giving them something safe to chew so they leave your houseplants alone.

What is cat grass?

Cat grass is usually wheatgrass, oat grass, or barley grass grown in a small tray. It provides fibre, aids digestion, and gives indoor cats a safe outlet for their plant-chewing instinct. You can grow it from seed in about 5-7 days, or buy pre-grown trays from most NZ pet stores and supermarkets.

How to use cat grass effectively

  • Keep it fresh: Cat grass lasts about 1-2 weeks before it goes yellow. Grow multiple trays and rotate them.
  • Place it strategically: Put the cat grass near the plants your cat is most likely to chew. If they have a safe alternative right there, they are less likely to go for the toxic one.
  • Let them graze: Do not restrict access. The point is that they always have a safe option available.
  • Catnip and catmint: Both are safe and provide enrichment. Catnip (Nepeta cataria) is a perennial that grows well in NZ. About 50% of cats respond to it.

How to Protect Pets from Toxic Plants

If you cannot bear to part with a plant, or you are caring for a pet in someone else home, here are ways to reduce the risk.

Indoor strategies

  • Remove toxic plants entirely. This is the only 100% effective strategy. Everything else is a risk reduction, not elimination.
  • Hang plants from the ceiling. Cats cannot reach what they cannot jump to. Hanging baskets work for trailing plants like string of hearts.
  • Use high shelves. Some cats will climb anything. Know your cat. If they are a climber, high shelves are not enough.
  • Bitter spray deterrents. Pet-safe bitter sprays (available at all NZ pet stores) make leaves taste unpleasant. They work for some cats and dogs but not all.
  • Aluminium foil on pots. Cats hate walking on foil. Line the top of the pot to stop them from digging in the soil.
  • Pebbles on soil. Large pebbles on top of potting mix prevent cats from digging and make the pot less appealing as a litter tray.
  • Provide cat grass. If they have a safe plant to chew, they are less likely to go for the dangerous ones.

Outdoor strategies

  • Fence off toxic plants. If you have rhododendrons, lilies, or other toxic plants in your garden, create a physical barrier around them.
  • Supervise in new gardens. When visiting friends or walking in parks, keep your dog on lead until you know what plants are in the area.
  • Check council plantings. Many NZ councils plant karaka trees as street trees. In autumn, the fallen fruit is extremely dangerous for dogs. Check your regular walking routes.
  • Train a solid leave it. The leave it command could save your dog life. Practice it with treats, then with interesting items on walks, then with actual plants.

What to Do If Your Pet Eats a Toxic Plant

Do not wait for symptoms. If you saw your pet eat a plant, or you find chewed leaves and suspect ingestion, act immediately.

1 Remove the plant material. Take it out of your pet mouth if you can do so safely. Do not put your fingers in a dog mouth if they are in distress.
2 Identify the plant. Take a photo or grab a sample. Knowing which plant it was determines the treatment.
3 Call your vet immediately. Do not wait for symptoms. Some toxins, especially lily toxins in cats, cause irreversible damage within hours. Tell the vet the plant name if you know it.
4 Do not induce vomiting unless your vet tells you to. Some plants cause more damage coming back up than going down. Corrosive plants are one example. Your vet will advise.
5 Follow your vet instructions. This may include bringing your pet in immediately, inducing vomiting at home with hydrogen peroxide (only if instructed), or monitoring for symptoms.
NZ Poisons Centre: 0800 764 766 (0800 POISON). Available 24/7. They can advise on plant toxicity for both humans and pets. Have the plant name ready if possible.

After-hours vet: If your regular vet is closed, search for emergency vet clinics in your area. In Auckland, call the Auckland Veterinary Emergency Clinic. In Wellington, call Wellington After Hours Veterinary Clinic. In Christchurch, call the Christchurch After Hours Veterinary Clinic. Save these numbers in your phone before you need them.
Pet sitter protocol: If you are leaving your pet with a sitter, provide a list of toxic plants in and around your home, the location of your nearest emergency vet, and clear instructions to call you and the vet immediately if they suspect plant ingestion. Key Takeaways

  • Lilies kill cats. If you have cats, remove all lilies from your home and garden. This is not negotiable. Pollen alone can cause fatal kidney failure.
  • Most popular houseplants are toxic. Peace lily, monstera, pothos, philodendron, dieffenbachia, aloe vera, ZZ plant, snake plant. If it is trending on Instagram, it is probably toxic.
  • Safe alternatives exist. Spider plants, Boston ferns, peperomia, parlour palms, and prayer plants are all safe, beautiful, and easy to find in NZ.
  • Karaka berries and tutu are NZ-specific dangers. Know what they look like and where they grow in your area.
  • Do not wait for symptoms. If your pet eats a plant, call your vet immediately. Early treatment makes the difference between a full recovery and permanent damage.
  • Tell your pet sitter. If you have toxic plants in your home, either remove them before the sit or clearly label them and provide emergency vet details.
