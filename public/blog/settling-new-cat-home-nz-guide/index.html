How to Settle a New Cat Into Your Home: Complete NZ Guide

6 August 2026 · 11 min read · By ZiggySitters Team

Bringing a new cat home is exciting — and stressful, for both of you. Whether you are adopting a kitten from a Wellington breeder, rescuing an adult cat from the SPCA, or welcoming a stray into your Auckland apartment, the first few weeks determine how well your cat adjusts to their new life. New Zealand's unique environment, from our native wildlife hazards to rental restrictions, adds local considerations that overseas guides do not cover.

This guide walks you through the entire settling-in process: from preparing your home before the cat arrives, through the critical first days, to full household integration. Whether you are a first-time cat owner or adding another feline to your family, this is the NZ-specific playbook you need.

Before You Bring Your Cat Home

Preparation makes the difference between a smooth transition and a stressful one. Get these sorted before the cat arrives:

Cat-Proof Your Home

New Zealand homes have specific hazards that require attention:

  • Toxic plants. NZ gardens are full of lilies (extremely toxic to cats — even the pollen can cause kidney failure), cyclamen, aloe vera, and kalanchoe. Remove or secure all lilies before your cat arrives. Check our NZ toxic plants guide for the full list.
  • Windows. Many NZ homes have push-out or sliding windows without screens. Cats can fall from upper-story windows — a real risk in Auckland and Wellington apartments. Install window screens or restrict openings to 5cm.
  • Cords and strings. Blind cords are a strangulation hazard. Use cord winders or cut looped cords. Dental floss, sewing thread, and rubber bands are also dangerous if swallowed.
  • Household chemicals. Store cleaning products, ant baits, and snail pellets in locked cupboards. NZ slug bait (metaldehyde) is highly toxic to cats and common in gardens.
  • Small spaces. Block access to gaps behind appliances, under decks, and inside reclining furniture. Cats in a new environment will seek hiding spots, and you do not want them trapped somewhere inaccessible.

Register with a Vet

Book a vet check within the first week. If you adopted from an SPCA or rescue, they usually include an initial vet visit. NZ vet clinics are well-distributed — find one near you and register before you need them. In an emergency, know the location of your nearest after-hours vet clinic. Auckland, Wellington, and Christchurch all have 24-hour emergency services.

Microchip and Registration

If your new cat is not already microchipped, arrange this at your first vet visit. The New Zealand Companion Animal Register (NZCAR) is the national microchip database — ensure your contact details are registered and up to date. Many SPCA and rescue cats come already microchipped and registered, but verify the details are transferred to your name.

Setting Up a Safe Room

This is the single most important step in the settling-in process. Do not let your new cat have free run of the house on day one.

The safe room is a single, quiet room where your cat can decompress, explore at their own pace, and feel secure. It should contain:

  • Food and water bowls — placed away from the litter box (cats dislike eating near their toilet). Use shallow bowls that do not touch their whiskers.
  • A litter box — unscented, clumping litter in an open tray. For kittens, use a low-sided tray they can climb into easily. One litter box per cat, plus one extra, is the standard recommendation.
  • Hiding spots — cardboard boxes, a covered cat bed, or space under a bed. Do not use spaces where you cannot reach your cat if needed.
  • A scratching post — vertical is preferred. Cats stretch and scratch when they wake up, so place it near their sleeping area.
  • A comfortable bed — but do not be surprised if your cat prefers a cardboard box. Cats choose their own beds.
  • Toys — wand toys, balls, and a cat tunnel. Avoid string toys unsupervised — they are a swallowing hazard.
  • A window to look out of — cats are naturally curious. A window view provides entertainment and reduces stress.
ZiggySitters Tip: Use a Feliway diffuser in the safe room. These synthetic pheromone diffusers (available from all NZ vet clinics and pet stores for approximately $50 to $70) help reduce stress and are particularly effective for cats adjusting to new environments. Plug it in 24 hours before your cat arrives.

The First 72 Hours

Day 1: Arrival and Decompression

  • Transport your cat in a secure carrier. Cover it with a light towel to reduce visual stress.
  • Carry the carrier directly to the safe room. Close the door before opening the carrier.
  • Open the carrier door and step back. Let the cat emerge when ready — this could take minutes or hours.
  • Do not force interaction. Sit quietly in the room reading or on your phone. Let the cat approach you.
  • Offer high-value food (wet food, Churu treats) near — but not on — your body. The cat learns that your presence means good things.
  • If the cat hides, that is normal. Do not pull them out of hiding. Place food, water, and the litter box near the hiding spot.

Days 2-3: Building Trust

  • Continue spending quiet time in the room. Read aloud softly — this helps the cat get used to your voice.
  • Offer treats by hand when the cat approaches. Let them sniff your hand before attempting to stroke them.
  • If the cat allows touching, stroke along the cheek and chin — these are scent gland areas that cats find reassuring.
  • Start interactive play with a wand toy. Play is one of the fastest ways to build trust because it redirects predatory energy and creates positive associations.
  • Monitor eating, drinking, and litter box use. A cat that is eating, drinking, and using the litter box is a cat that is settling, even if they are still hiding.
Red flag: If your cat has not eaten or used the litter box within 24 hours, contact your vet. Stress can cause cats to stop eating, which can lead to hepatic lipidosis (a dangerous liver condition) within 48 to 72 hours. This is a medical emergency, not a behavioural issue.

Settling-In Timeline: What to Expect

Days 1-3: Hiding is normal. Cat may not eat much, may not explore. Stay calm, stay consistent. Check food and water levels twice daily. Use Feliway.
Days 3-7: Cat begins exploring the safe room. May start to approach you voluntarily. Eating and litter habits should be normalising. Play sessions become longer.
Days 7-14: Cat is comfortable in the safe room and may be curious about what is beyond the door. This is when you can start opening the door to let them explore the rest of the house — one room at a time.
Weeks 2-4: Cat has established territory. Personality starts to emerge. You see playfulness, affection, and routine. This is the "settling in" phase — the cat is home.
Weeks 4-8: Full adjustment. The cat is confident, exploring, and showing their true personality. Multi-cat households may still be working through introductions at this stage.

Shy, anxious, or rescue cats may take longer — sometimes 8 to 12 weeks for full adjustment. That is normal. The timeline is a guide, not a deadline. Pushing a cat faster than they are ready creates setbacks that take longer to resolve than going slowly in the first place.

Introducing Your New Cat to Other Pets

This is where most people go wrong — rushing the introduction. The process should take at least 1 to 2 weeks for dogs and 2 to 4 weeks for other cats.

Introducing to Another Cat

  1. Separate rooms, separate everything (Days 1-3). Each cat has their own safe room with their own food, water, litter, and resting areas. Do not let them see each other yet.
  2. Scent swapping (Days 3-5). Rub a clean cloth on each cat's cheek, then place the cloth near the other cat's food bowl. This teaches them that the other cat's scent is associated with positive things. Also swap their bedding between rooms.
  3. Visual contact through a barrier (Days 5-7). Crack the safe room door or use a baby gate. Allow brief, supervised visual contact. If either cat shows aggression (hissing, growling, swatting), close the door and try again the next day.
  4. Supervised meetings (Days 7-10). Allow both cats in the same room for short periods (5 to 10 minutes). Have treats ready to reward calm behaviour. Distract with wand toys if tension builds.
  5. Gradual integration (Weeks 2-4). Increase unsupervised time gradually. Continue separate feeding areas and litter boxes. The rule is one litter box per cat, plus one extra — always.

Introducing to a Dog

Dogs and cats can absolutely coexist peacefully, but the introduction requires more management because of the size difference and prey drive.

  • Never leave them unsupervised until you are 100% confident the dog will not chase the cat.
  • Keep the dog on a lead during initial introductions. Reward the dog for calm, disinterested behaviour around the cat.
  • Give the cat high escape routes — cat shelves, tall scratching posts, or access to rooms the dog cannot reach. The cat must always have a safe retreat.
  • Feed separately. Food is a major trigger for both species. Feed the cat on a high surface the dog cannot reach.
  • Be patient with high-prey-drive breeds. Terriers, greyhounds, and working breeds may take longer to accept a cat. Professional behaviourist guidance is worthwhile for these combinations.

NZ-Specific Considerations for New Cat Owners

New Zealand has unique factors that affect cat ownership differently from other countries:

Cat Containment Laws

New Zealand does not yet have national cat containment requirements, but several councils are moving in that direction. Wellington City Council requires cats to be microchipped and registered, and some regional councils are discussing 24-hour cat curfews. The SPCA NZ recommends keeping cats indoors or in enclosed outdoor areas (catios) to protect both native wildlife and the cats themselves. Our NZ cat containment guide covers current and proposed regulations.

Native Wildlife Protection

New Zealand's native birds — kiwi, kakariki, kereru, tui — are vulnerable to cat predation. Responsible cat owners keep their cats indoors at dawn and dusk (when birds are most active), use bells on collars, and consider catios or enclosed outdoor runs. If you live near a predator-free sanctuary or native bush area, be especially vigilant about containment.

Rental Properties

NZ's rental market can be challenging for pet owners. The 2024 tenancy law changes gave tenants more rights to request pets, but landlords can still impose reasonable conditions. When settling a new cat into a rental:

  • Get written permission before bringing a cat into a rental property.
  • Offer a "pet bond" or pet agreement if the landlord requests one.
  • Use scratching posts rather than allowing cats to scratch furniture or carpets.
  • Keep the litter box on a waterproof mat to protect floors.
  • Document the property condition before and after the cat arrives.

NZ-Safe Cat Food and Products

NZ pet food regulations are governed by the Ministry for Primary Industries (MPI). Choose commercial cat food that meets AAFCO standards — all major NZ brands (Royal Canin, Hill's, Whiskas, Fancy Feast) meet these requirements. Avoid raw feeding without veterinary guidance, as NZ raw meat can carry bacteria and parasites. For treats, NZ-made options like Ziwi Peak and K9 Natural are high quality and locally available.

When Things Are Not Going Well

Not every settling-in goes smoothly. Here are the most common problems and what to do:

Cat Will Not Eat

Some stress-related appetite loss is normal for the first 24 to 48 hours. Offer high-value wet food, warm it slightly to increase aroma, and try different flavours. If the cat has not eaten for more than 48 hours, see your vet immediately — hepatic lipidosis can develop quickly.

Cat Will Not Use the Litter Box

Common causes in a new home: the litter box is too close to food, the litter type is different from what the cat is used to, or the cat feels unsafe accessing the box. Try a different litter type (unscented clumping clay is the most widely accepted), move the box away from food, and ensure the cat can access it without being cornered. If the cat is urinating outside the box, rule out a urinary tract infection with your vet first — stress can trigger FLUTD (feline lower urinary tract disease).

Cat Is Hiding and Will Not Come Out

Give it time. Some cats take 3 to 5 days just to feel safe enough to explore one room. Continue spending quiet time in the room. Do not reach into hiding spots or force interaction. The cat will come out when they feel safe. If hiding persists beyond a week with no improvement, consult your vet or a feline behaviourist.

Aggression Between Cats

Go back a step in the introduction process. If cats are fighting, separate them immediately and return to the scent-swapping phase for at least a week before trying visual contact again. Feliway Friends (different from regular Feliway) is designed for multi-cat tension and can help. If aggression persists, consult a veterinary behaviourist — early intervention prevents long-term problems.

Using a Pet Sitter During the Settling Period

If you need to travel or be away from home during your cat's first few weeks, a professional pet sitter who understands the settling-in process is invaluable. Here is what to look for and prepare:

  • Choose a sitter experienced with new cats. Not all pet sitters understand the nuances of a cat that is still adjusting. Look for sitters who ask about your cat's settling stage and have a plan for maintaining routine.
  • Prepare a detailed care sheet. Include feeding schedule, food type and quantity, litter box location and cleaning routine, vet contact details, your cat's current settling stage, and specific behaviours to watch for.
  • Introduce the sitter before you leave. Arrange a meet-and-greet so your cat can smell and see the sitter while you are present. This reduces the stress of a stranger entering the safe room when you are gone.
  • Ask for daily updates with photos. A good pet sitter will send daily updates including food intake, litter box use, and your cat's behaviour. This information is critical during the settling period.
  • Maintain the routine. The sitter should feed, play, and interact with your cat at the same times you do. Consistency is key during the settling-in period.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long does it take for a new cat to settle into a home?

Most cats take 2 to 4 weeks to fully settle into a new home, but the timeline varies. Shy or anxious cats may need 6 to 8 weeks or longer. Kittens typically adjust faster (3 to 7 days) than adult cats. Rescue cats from shelters often need more time, especially if they have had multiple homes. The key is patience — do not rush the process.

Should I keep my new cat in one room at first?

Yes. Set up a single safe room with everything your cat needs: food, water, litter box, hiding spots, a scratching post, and a comfortable bed. This gives your cat a secure base to explore from. Once your cat is eating, drinking, and using the litter box confidently — usually after 3 to 7 days — you can gradually open up more of the house.

My new cat is hiding and will not come out. What should I do?

This is normal for the first few days. Place food, water, and the litter box near the hiding spot. Sit quietly in the room without trying to engage the cat. Speak softly. Let the cat approach you when they are ready. Most cats start exploring within 2 to 5 days. If hiding persists beyond a week with no improvement, consult your vet.

Can I use a pet sitter while my new cat is settling in?

Yes, but choose a sitter experienced with new cats. Introduce the sitter before you leave so your cat can get used to their scent. Provide a detailed care sheet with the settling-in stage, feeding routine, and any specific instructions. Maintain the daily routine as closely as possible. What are the signs a new cat is settling in well?

Positive signs include eating and drinking normally, using the litter box, grooming, exploring the room, playing with toys, showing curiosity about you, slow blinking, kneading, purring, and sleeping in the open rather than hiding. If your cat is doing most of these after the first week, the settling process is going well.
